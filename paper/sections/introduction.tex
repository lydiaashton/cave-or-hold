\section{Introduction}

An AI-generated analysis should reflect what the data support, even when the user expects a different result. Herda\u{g}delen et al.\ (2026) found that agents' preferences influenced experts' analytical choices. We ask whether influence also runs the other way. Does a user's expectation shift the model's conclusion, and does its timing matter?

A user's stated expectation may lead an analytical summary to claim more than the evidence supports. Ashton (2026a, 2026b) describes confidence laundering as AI packaging its output in the format and register of valid analysis, regardless of the foundation, and identifies sycophancy as an amplifier. Sharma et al.\ (2024) found that incorrect user suggestions reduced model accuracy on factual questions. Framing in the first request is known to shift analytical conclusions (Allen \& Peterson, 2026; Balani \& Panda, 2026); we ask whether an expectation that arrives after the model has analyzed the data does the same. A model may perform the calculations correctly yet conclude that those calculations do not justify.\footnote{In earlier exploratory work (Ashton, 2026b), a small benchmark of 4 tasks was built with Claude Code, each containing a trap: a feature, such as a weak instrument, on which a default analysis gives the wrong answer. Across 12 runs the model's estimates were correct on every trap, but by the researcher's blind reading 6 of 12 write-ups claimed more than the analysis supported.}

In practice, the person who asks for an analysis does not know the right answer; that is why they asked. A conclusion that drifts toward their expectation then reads as confirmation, and no one in the conversation can tell. Measuring this requires a setting where the analysis is real, but the correct conclusion is known to the experimenter and hidden from both the user and the model. Real datasets lack the ground truth, and fixed summaries remove the analysis.

We compare final summaries when an expectation is stated before analysis, introduced after the first draft, or withheld. Each simulated dataset has a reference conclusion fixed before the runs. Human scorers, blind to condition and the reference answer, code the summary's claims and uncertainty. A pre-specified mapping determines whether those codes indicate an error in the direction of the user's expectation. We say that a bottom line (the summary's concluding claim for the board) that errs toward the expectation has caved, and one that stays with the evidence has held. The After and Never conditions share the preceding analysis and first draft, allowing us to examine the effect of introducing an expectation at finalization.

Stated after the first draft, the expectation raised the share of bottom lines that erred toward it from 6 to 13 of 60 (10\% to 22\%), and all 7 discordant pairs moved toward the expectation (exact McNemar \(p = .016\)). Whether stating it before the analysis makes a difference remains open at this sample size (11 against 5 discordant pairs, \(p = .21\)). Three LLM judges agreed closely with the human panel and reproduced both conclusions. In the later, exploratory turns, the user asked: ``Are you sure about the bottom line?'' After that question, fewer bottom lines erred in the underpowered world and more erred in the strong world. In the confounded world, most bottom lines erred before any expectation was stated.

Our main contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A matched design that isolates the expectation.} Each After run continues its own Never run, so the pair shares one analysis; prior analysis-task designs we know of place the pressure in the first request.

    \item \textbf{Evidence that one sentence with no argument behind it can make a model cave.} Stated after the model's analysis, it turned held bottom lines into caved ones in 7 matched pairs and never the reverse.

    \item \textbf{Measuring caving when no one knows the answer.} Blind scorers code the bottom line against an answer fixed by design; three LLM judges, which never see that answer, agreed closely with them.

    \item \textbf{A pre-registered, reusable protocol.} Design, sample size and analysis were registered before scoring, and the code, data, prompts and labels are released.
\end{itemize}
