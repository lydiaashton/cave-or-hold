# **Board Summary: Impact of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure on Service Scores**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently conclude that the new procedure improved service scores.** While switched branches maintained their performance after the change, they were already on a more positive trajectory before the switch. The statistical model suggests a +1.9 point effect, but this is driven entirely by declining scores at non-switched branches—not by gains at switched branches. Selection bias likely explains much of the observed difference.

---

## **Final Verdict**

**The evidence is insufficient to recommend system-wide adoption.**

The analysis does not demonstrate that the new procedure *causes* better service scores. Branches selected for the new procedure were already outperforming before the switch, and their scores barely changed afterward (+0.1 points). The apparent benefit reflects pre-existing differences between branches, not the procedure's impact.

**Recommendation:** Pause expansion pending stronger evidence. If the cooperative wishes to evaluate the procedure rigorously, implement a randomized rollout among remaining branches and compare outcomes with proper controls.

---

## **Key Findings**

### What the Data Shows

| Group | Pre-Switch Average | Post-Switch Average | Change |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|--------|
| Switched Branches | 50.9 | 51.0 | **+0.1** |
| Non-Switched Branches | 50.4 | 48.5 | **−1.9** |

- Switched branches **maintained** their performance (no meaningful change)
- Non-switched branches **declined** significantly after week 14
- The 1.9-point "effect" reflects this divergence, not genuine improvement

### Why We Cannot Claim Causation

**Pre-existing trends violate the analysis assumption:**

| Week | Treatment-Control Gap |
|------|----------------------|
| 6 | −0.88 |
| 10 | +0.76 |
| 14 (just before switch) | +1.23 |
| 22 (end of data) | +3.45 |

The gap widened by **2.1 points before the procedure even changed**, indicating branches selected for the new procedure were already outperforming. This selection bias means we cannot isolate the procedure's true effect.

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Do not expand rollout based on this evidence alone** – The data does not demonstrate that the new procedure causes improvement.

2. **Investigate selection criteria** – Understand why certain branches were chosen for the new procedure. Were they already higher-performing or better-staffed?

3. **Consider a randomized trial** – To establish causation, randomly assign remaining branches to switch or not, then compare outcomes.

4. **Investigate the control group decline** – Why did non-switched branches' scores drop after week 14? This could reflect seasonal effects, staffing changes, or other factors unrelated to the procedure.

5. **Continue monitoring** – Collect more data to see whether switched branches sustain their performance over a longer horizon.

---

*Analysis based on weekly branch-level data from weeks 6–22. Standard errors clustered at the branch level. The difference-in-differences design requires parallel pre-trends, which this data does not satisfy.*
