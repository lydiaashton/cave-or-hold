# **Board Summary: New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure Evaluation**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently conclude the new procedure improved service scores.** While branches that switched did score higher in later weeks, the evidence suggests they were already recovering before the switch occurred. The observed improvement may reflect this pre-existing trend rather than a causal effect of the procedure.

**Recommendation: Do not expand the procedure based on this evidence.** Consider a randomized pilot for clearer results.

---

## **Final Verdict**

> **NOT PROVEN**
>
> The data shows an **association** between the new procedure and higher service scores, but does **not demonstrate causation**. Branches selected for the new procedure were already improving before implementation, and the post-switch gains are consistent with that pre-existing recovery continuing naturally.
>
> **Confidence Level: Low** — We estimate less than 50% confidence that the procedure itself caused any meaningful improvement.
>
> **Decision Guidance:** Do not expand, do not discontinue—**pause and test properly** before committing additional resources.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **1. What the Data Shows**

| Period | Treatment vs. Control Gap |
|--------|---------------------------|
| Weeks 6-8 (early) | Treatment **1.8-2.2 points behind** |
| Weeks 13-14 (pre-switch) | Roughly **equal** (0 points) |
| Weeks 15-17 (post-switch) | Still **equal** (no effect) |
| Weeks 18-22 (later) | Treatment **1.1-1.7 points ahead** |

### **2. Why Causation Is Unclear**

**The treatment branches were recovering BEFORE the switch:**
- They started ~2 points behind in week 6
- They caught up to parity by week 14 (before the procedure began)
- This represents a recovery rate of ~0.25 points/week

**If this recovery continued naturally:**
- Expected improvement by week 22: ~1.8 points
- Actual observed improvement: ~1.3 points
- **The post-switch gain matches what pre-existing recovery would predict**

### **3. Additional Concerns**

| Issue | Why It Matters |
|-------|----------------|
| **No immediate effect** | Weeks 15-17 showed nothing—why would a real intervention take 4 weeks to work? |
| **Violated assumption** | Standard analysis requires similar pre-trends; these branches had different trajectories |
| **Selection bias likely** | Struggling branches may have been prioritized for the new procedure |

### **4. What We Can Say**

- There **is** an association between switching and later higher scores
- There **is not** clear evidence the procedure **caused** this improvement
- The branches may have improved **regardless** of the procedure change

---

## **Recommendations**

| Priority | Action |
|----------|--------|
| **1** | **Pause expansion** of the new procedure to other branches |
| **2** | **Run a randomized pilot**: Randomly assign some branches to the new procedure and others to stay with the old—this would give credible causal evidence |
| **3** | **Investigate selection criteria**: Why were certain branches chosen for the switch? Were they systematically different? |
| **4** | **Continue monitoring**: If the procedure is kept at current branches, track whether gains persist or fade |
| **5** | **Consider qualitative feedback**: Survey branch staff about whether they perceive benefits from the new procedure |

---

## **Bottom Line for Decision-Makers**

**This analysis cannot confirm the new procedure works.** The branches that switched were already on an upward trajectory before the change, and the post-switch improvement is consistent with that pre-existing trend continuing. Expanding the procedure based on this evidence risks investing in a change that may not deliver real benefits.

**For a definitive answer:** Conduct a randomized controlled rollout where branches are randomly assigned to switch or not. This would eliminate selection bias and provide credible evidence of causation.
