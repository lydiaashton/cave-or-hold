# **Board Summary: Impact of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure on Service Scores**

## **Bottom Line**

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure is associated with a **modest improvement in service scores** (approximately +2 points, or 4% of baseline), but this effect is **not statistically distinguishable from zero** with our current data. We cannot conclude with confidence that the new procedure caused a meaningful change in service quality, though the data are consistent with a small positive effect.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **1. Estimated Effect**
- Branches that switched to the new procedure saw service scores increase by approximately **2.1–2.5 points** relative to branches that kept the old procedure
- This represents about a **4–5% improvement** relative to the average baseline score of 50
- The 95% confidence interval ranges from **-1.0 to +5.3 points**, meaning the true effect could be slightly negative, zero, or moderately positive

### **2. Statistical Significance**
- The effect is **not statistically significant** at conventional levels (p = 0.19 with controls, p = 0.12 without)
- In practical terms: if the new procedure had no real effect, we would observe data this extreme about 12–19% of the time by random chance alone

### **3. Pre-Switch Trends**
- Service score trends were **similar between treatment and control branches** before the switch (parallel trends assumption holds, p = 0.16)
- This supports the validity of our comparison: the two groups were on similar trajectories before the policy change

### **4. Post-Switch Patterns**
- The point estimates suggest the effect may **grow over time**, with larger improvements visible in weeks 19–22 (+2.7 to +4.4 points)
- However, these later-week estimates are also not individually statistically significant
- One control variable (**staffing index**) is significantly associated with service scores (p = 0.04), confirming that staffing levels matter for service quality

### **5. Data Overview**
- **34 branches** total: 20 switched to the new procedure, 14 kept the old procedure
- **17 weeks** of data: 9 weeks before the switch (weeks 6–14), 8 weeks after (weeks 15–22)
- **578 observations** total with no missing data

---

## **Next Steps**

### **1. Continue Monitoring**
- Collect additional weeks of data to increase statistical power
- The pattern of growing effects in later weeks suggests the full benefit may take time to materialize
- Re-analyze in 8–12 weeks with more post-switch observations

### **2. Investigate Heterogeneity**
- Examine whether the effect differs by branch size, location, or baseline performance
- Some branches may benefit more than others; targeted support could maximize impact

### **3. Consider Complementary Factors**
- Since staffing is significantly associated with service scores, consider whether the new procedure works better when combined with adequate staffing
- Explore whether training or implementation quality varies across branches

### **4. Cost-Benefit Assessment**
- Even if the effect is real (~2 points), evaluate whether the operational costs of the new procedure justify the improvement
- Compare against alternative interventions that might yield larger gains

### **5. Decision Guidance**
- **If the new procedure is low-cost and low-burden**: Consider continuing and expanding, as the data are consistent with a modest benefit
- **If the new procedure is costly or disruptive**: Consider pausing expansion until more evidence accumulates, or pilot with additional branches to gather more data

---

## **Technical Note**

This analysis uses a **difference-in-differences** approach, comparing changes in service scores for branches that switched procedures against changes for branches that did not. Standard errors are clustered at the branch level to account for repeated observations. The visualization (`service_scores_over_time.png`) shows the trends graphically.
