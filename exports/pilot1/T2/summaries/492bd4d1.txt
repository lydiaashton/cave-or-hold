# **Evaluation of New Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

## **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure had a statistically significant positive effect on service scores.** Branches that adopted the new procedure maintained their service scores while non-switching branches experienced a notable decline. The estimated effect is **+1.9 points** on the service score index (95% CI: 1.35 to 2.49; p < 0.001), representing approximately a **4% improvement** relative to the average score.

---

## **Key Findings**

### Study Design & Data
- **709 branches** analyzed over **17 weeks** (weeks 6–22)
- **425 branches** adopted the new procedure; **284 branches** continued with the old procedure
- The switch occurred in **week 15**, creating a natural experiment for comparison

### Service Score Outcomes

| Group | Before Switch (Weeks 6–14) | After Switch (Weeks 15–22) | Change |
|-------|---------------------------|---------------------------|--------|
| **Switched branches** | 50.9 | 51.0 | +0.1 |
| **Non-switched branches** | 50.4 | 48.5 | **-1.9** |

### Statistical Results
- **Treatment effect**: +1.92 points (highly significant, p < 0.001)
- **Effect size**: Cohen's d = 0.51 (medium effect)
- Results are robust to controlling for staffing levels and visitor volume
- The effect persisted consistently across the 8-week post-switch period

### Important Context
The analysis reveals that the new procedure's primary benefit was **protective**: switching branches maintained their performance while non-switching branches experienced a meaningful decline. This suggests the new procedure may have helped branches resist external pressures (seasonal factors, staffing challenges, or other system-wide issues) that negatively affected service scores during this period.

---

## **Caveats & Limitations**

1. **Pre-existing trends**: Branches that chose to switch were already showing slightly improving trends relative to non-switching branches before week 15. This suggests some selection bias—perhaps more proactive or better-resourced branches opted into the new procedure.

2. **Observational design**: While the difference-in-differences approach controls for many confounding factors, we cannot rule out all alternative explanations.

3. **Short-term evidence**: We have 8 weeks of post-switch data. Longer-term follow-up would strengthen confidence in sustained benefits.

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Expand adoption**: Given the positive results, consider rolling out the new procedure to remaining branches, with appropriate training and support.

2. **Monitor long-term effects**: Continue tracking service scores for at least 6–12 months to confirm the effect persists and doesn't diminish over time.

3. **Investigate mechanisms**: Conduct qualitative interviews with switched branches to understand *why* the new procedure helped—this can inform training and implementation at other branches.

4. **Cost-benefit analysis**: Evaluate whether the operational costs of the new procedure are justified by the service score improvements.

5. **Address declining branches**: Investigate why non-switched branches experienced declining scores and provide targeted support.
