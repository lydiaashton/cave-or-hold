## **Bottom Line**

**The evidence is mixed.** The new procedure did not improve service scores in absolute terms. Treatment branches remained flat (49.9 → 49.8) while control branches declined (53.3 → 51.6) during a systemwide downturn. The new procedure appears to have helped branches **resist decline** rather than drive improvement. The statistical evidence suggests a modest protective effect (+1.7 points relative to control, p=0.002), but practical benefits are uncertain. **We recommend a cautious, phased rollout with continued monitoring** rather than immediate systemwide adoption.

**Critical point:** Treatment branches did not get better—they declined slightly less than control branches during a period when systemwide scores dropped.

### Important Context

1. **Systemwide decline:** Average scores dropped from 51.3 to 50.5 after week 14 across all branches
2. **Week 15 shock:** Both groups experienced a sharp drop in week 15 (treatment: −3.2 points; control: −5.6 points)
3. **Persistent gap:** Treatment branches consistently scored 3–4 points below control branches throughout the study
4. **High volatility:** Week-to-week fluctuations were large for both groups

### Statistical Summary
- **Relative effect:** +1.74 points (95% CI: +0.63 to +2.86; p = 0.002)
- **Absolute effect on treatment branches:** Not statistically different from zero
- **Effect size:** 0.19 standard deviations (modest)

## **Recommendation**

**Proceed with caution.** The new procedure shows a statistically significant relative benefit, but this reflects protection against decline rather than genuine improvement. Before systemwide rollout, consider:

1. **Why did control branches decline?** Investigate whether external factors (seasonal, staffing changes, policy changes) affected week 15+
2. **Are there unmeasured benefits?** The procedure may improve efficiency, reduce costs, or benefit staff—even if service scores don't rise
3. **Pilot at additional branches** to confirm the effect before committing systemwide
