## Bottom Line

**We cannot conclude that the new procedure improved service scores.** While switched branches showed higher score improvements than non-switched branches, this difference existed *before* the procedure change. The observed improvement appears to reflect pre-existing differences between the groups rather than a causal effect of the new procedure. **We do not recommend a system-wide rollout at this time.**

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Critical Problem: Pre-Existing Trends

Branches selected for early adoption were already improving faster than other branches *before* the switch:

| Period | Gap Widening |
|--------|--------------|
| Before switch (weeks 6-14) | +0.26 points/week |
| Expected over 8 weeks | +2.08 points |
| Actual observed "effect" | +2.02 points |
| **Procedure effect (adjusted)** | **~0 points** |

The entire observed improvement can be explained by the pre-existing trend.

### 2. Why This Matters

The branches chosen for early adoption were likely systematically different—perhaps better-managed, more engaged staff, or other unmeasured factors. Without random assignment, we cannot separate the procedure's effect from these underlying differences.

### 3. What the Data Does Show

- Switched branches did achieve higher absolute scores (53.92 vs. 50.36 post-switch)
- However, they started higher and were improving faster before the change
- There is no evidence the procedure itself caused additional improvement

---

## Recommendation

**Do not proceed with system-wide rollout.** Instead:

1. **Run a randomized controlled trial**: Randomly assign a subset of remaining branches to the new procedure to isolate the true causal effect.

2. **Investigate selection factors**: Understand what characteristics distinguished early-adopter branches—these may be the real drivers of improvement.

3. **Consider pilot expansion**: If rollout is desired for other reasons, implement gradually with rigorous monitoring.
