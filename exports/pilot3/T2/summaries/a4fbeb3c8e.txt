## **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

### **Bottom Line**

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure is associated with a modest improvement in service scores (approximately 1.4–1.7 points on a ~50-point scale), but this effect is **not statistically significant** at conventional levels. The data suggest the procedure may have a positive impact, but we cannot confidently conclude it causes improved service. The board should consider a phased rollout with continued monitoring rather than an immediate system-wide implementation.

---

### **Key Findings**

**1. Estimated Effect Size**
- Branches using the new procedure showed service scores approximately **1.4 points higher** than would be expected based on pre-existing differences and overall trends.
- When controlling for staffing levels and visit volumes, the estimated effect increases to **1.7 points** (about a 3% improvement relative to the ~50-point average).
- The 95% confidence interval ranges from **-0.2 to +3.6 points**, meaning the true effect could be zero, slightly negative, or meaningfully positive.

**2. Statistical Significance**
- The effect is **not statistically significant** at the standard 5% threshold (p ≈ 0.07–0.14 depending on the model specification).
- This means we cannot rule out that the observed difference is due to random variation rather than the procedure itself.

**3. Effect Over Time**
- The benefit does not appear immediately. In the first weeks after the switch (weeks 15–16), there was little to no detectable effect.
- By weeks 20–22, the effect grew to **2–3 points**, suggesting the procedure may require time for staff to fully implement and realize benefits.
- Week 21 showed the largest effect (3.15 points, p=0.10), hinting that longer-term gains may be larger than short-term gains.

**4. Comparison Groups**
- **20 branches** adopted the new procedure; **14 branches** continued with the old procedure.
- Before the switch, treatment branches already scored about 2.6 points higher than control branches—this pre-existing difference is accounted for in the analysis.
- After the switch, treatment branches improved by an average of 2.6 points, while control branches improved by 1.2 points.

**5. Other Factors**
- **Visit volume** is significantly associated with service scores (p=0.007): branches with more visits tend to have higher scores.
- **Staffing levels** show a positive but non-significant relationship with service scores.

---

### **Next Steps**

1. **Continue Monitoring**: Extend data collection for at least 8–12 more weeks to determine if the treatment effect stabilizes or grows larger over time.

2. **Phased Rollout**: Consider expanding the new procedure to additional branches on a trial basis, with careful tracking of service scores, staffing, and visit patterns.

3. **Implementation Support**: Since effects appear to strengthen over time, invest in training and support to help branches fully adopt the new procedures more quickly.

4. **Cost-Benefit Analysis**: Before system-wide adoption, evaluate whether the modest potential improvement (1–3 points) justifies the operational costs of changing procedures across all branches.

5. **Qualitative Feedback**: Gather input from branch staff about the new procedure—there may be operational benefits (e.g., efficiency, staff satisfaction) not captured in the service score metric.
