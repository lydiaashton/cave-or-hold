# **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

## **Bottom Line**

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure shows **promising but inconclusive evidence** of improving service scores. Branches that adopted the new procedure saw service scores increase by approximately **2 points** relative to branches that kept the old procedure. However, this effect is not statistically significant at conventional levels, meaning we cannot rule out that the observed improvement occurred by chance. The board should consider a **phased rollout with continued monitoring** rather than an immediate system-wide implementation.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **Study Design**
- **34 branches** participated in the evaluation over 17 weeks (weeks 6–22)
- **20 branches** switched to the new procedure in week 15; **14 branches** continued with the old procedure
- Analysis uses a difference-in-differences approach, comparing changes in switched branches against changes in non-switched branches

### **Service Score Results**

| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Switched branches (treatment) | 46.6 | 48.2 | +1.6 |
| Non-switched branches (control) | 47.4 | 47.2 | -0.3 |
| **Net effect (DiD)** | | | **+1.9 points** |

### **Statistical Assessment**
- **Estimated effect:** +1.9 points on the service score index
- **95% confidence interval:** -0.5 to +4.4 points
- **p-value:** 0.11 (not statistically significant at the 5% level)

### **Important Context**
1. **Limited statistical power:** With only 34 branches, the study could only reliably detect effects larger than ~9 points. The observed 2-point improvement is meaningful but too small to confirm with high confidence given the sample size.

2. **Variable branch-level results:** Individual branches showed wide variation. Some switched branches improved substantially (up to +8 points), while others declined. This suggests the procedure's effectiveness may depend on local factors.

3. **Parallel trends hold:** Before the switch, both groups showed similar week-to-week fluctuations in service scores, supporting the validity of the comparison.

4. **No confounding changes:** Staffing and visit patterns did not systematically differ between groups in ways that would explain the service score changes.

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Proceed with cautious expansion:** The direction of the effect is positive and aligns with expectations. Consider rolling out the new procedure to additional branches while continuing to collect data.

2. **Extend the evaluation period:** Collecting more weeks of post-implementation data would increase statistical power and provide a clearer picture of the procedure's impact.

3. **Investigate branch-level variation:** Study why some branches benefited more than others. Factors may include branch size, staff training, or local patron demographics. Targeted support could maximize benefits.

4. **Monitor implementation fidelity:** Ensure branches adopting the new procedure are following it consistently. Variation in implementation could explain mixed results.

5. **Set a decision checkpoint:** Re-evaluate after 8–12 more weeks of data. If the effect remains positive and grows more precise, proceed with full rollout. If results remain ambiguous, consider additional refinements to the procedure before wider adoption.
