## **Analysis of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

### **Bottom Line**

The new procedure is associated with a **modest improvement** in service scores of approximately **2 points** on the 50-point scale. However, this effect is **not statistically significant at conventional levels** (p ≈ 0.11), meaning we cannot rule out that the observed improvement occurred by chance. The evidence suggests a positive direction but is not strong enough to confidently recommend a system-wide rollout based on service scores alone.

---

### **Key Findings**

**1. Study Design**
- 34 branches tracked over 17 weeks (weeks 6–22)
- 20 branches adopted the new procedure; 14 continued with the old procedure
- The switch occurred at week 15
- Analysis uses a difference-in-differences approach, comparing changes in switched branches against non-switched branches

**2. Service Score Impact**
| Group | Pre-Switch Average | Post-Switch Average | Change |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|--------|
| Switched branches | 49.7 | 53.0 | +3.3 |
| Non-switched branches | 51.0 | 52.2 | +1.2 |
| **Net effect (DiD)** | | | **+2.15** |

- The new procedure is associated with a **2.15-point increase** in service scores
- This effect remains stable (~2.06 points) when controlling for staffing levels and visitor volume
- **Statistical confidence**: p-value ≈ 0.11 (marginally significant; conventional threshold is p < 0.05)

**3. Important Context**
- Branches that switched had **lower baseline scores** than control branches (about 1.3 points lower before the switch)
- Both groups improved after week 15, but switched branches improved slightly more
- **Visitor volume** is a strong predictor of service scores—branches with more visitors tend to score higher, regardless of procedure

**4. Variation Across Branches**
- Not all switched branches benefited equally: improvements ranged from negative to +10.8 points
- Some control branches also showed substantial improvement without any procedure change
- This suggests other factors (local conditions, staffing, visitor patterns) play a significant role

---

### **Next Steps**

**1. Do Not Proceed with Immediate System-Wide Rollout**
The current evidence does not provide sufficient confidence that the new procedure causes meaningful improvement. A full rollout would be premature.

**2. Extend the Observation Period**
- Continue monitoring for at least 8–12 more weeks to determine if the effect strengthens or diminishes over time
- Short-term effects may differ from sustained impacts

**3. Investigate Heterogeneity**
- Analyze which types of branches benefited most (by size, location, staffing levels, visitor volume)
- Consider whether the procedure works better in specific contexts

**4. Complement with Qualitative Feedback**
- Survey branch staff about operational impacts (efficiency, workload, satisfaction)
- Service scores are one metric; staff experience and operational costs matter too

**5. Consider a Staged Rollout with Evaluation**
- If proceeding, implement in phases with rigorous tracking
- Establish clear success metrics and decision points for continuation

### **Technical Notes**

- Analysis uses difference-in-differences regression with branch and week fixed effects
- Standard errors clustered at the branch level to account for within-branch correlation
- Results are robust to controlling for staffing index and visits index
- Parallel trends assumption appears reasonable: pre-switch trends were relatively stable between groups
