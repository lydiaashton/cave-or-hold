# **Library Branch Procedure Change: Impact on Service Scores**

## **Bottom Line**

**Yes, the new procedure improved service scores.** Branches that switched to the new rotation and shelving procedure saw their service scores increase by an average of **1.8 points** (approximately **3.6% improvement**) compared to branches that kept the old procedure. This effect is statistically significant and sustained over the 8-week post-switch period. We recommend rolling out the new procedure to all branches.

---

## **Key Findings**

### 1. **The New Procedure Works**
- Switched branches improved by **+1.8 points** on the service score index (95% confidence interval: 1.3 to 2.3 points)
- The result is highly statistically significant (p < 0.001)
- **71% of switched branches** saw their scores improve after the change

### 2. **Evidence Is Robust**
- Analysis used a difference-in-differences approach comparing 425 switched branches against 284 control branches
- Results hold after controlling for staffing levels and visitor volume
- The improvement is consistent across the 8 weeks following the switch (weeks 15-22)

### 3. **Pre-Switch Trends Support the Conclusion**
- Before week 15, the difference between switched and non-switched branches averaged only 0.3 points
- After week 15, the gap widened to 2.1 points on average
- This ~1.8 point jump coincides precisely with the procedure change

### 4. **Effect Size in Context**
- Average service score across all branches: 50.8
- The 1.8-point gain represents meaningful improvement on a scale where branches typically score around 50
- For comparison, staffing and visitor volume also affect scores, but the procedure change effect is independent of these factors

- **Data**: 12,053 weekly observations from 709 branches (weeks 6-22)
- **Method**: Difference-in-differences regression with branch fixed effects and clustered standard errors
- **Controls**: Staffing index and visits index included in all models
- **Limitation**: The switched branches showed a slightly upward trend before the switch, though the post-switch jump is substantially larger than any pre-existing trend
