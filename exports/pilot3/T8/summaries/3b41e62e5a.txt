# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

**TO:** Library Cooperative Board  
**FROM:** Data Analysis Team  
**DATE:** November 14, 2026  
**SUBJECT:** Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure

---

## **BOTTOM LINE**

**We cannot confidently conclude the new procedure improved service scores.** While branches that adopted the new procedure showed larger score increases than those that didn't, **this gap was already widening before the switch occurred**. The treatment branches were already improving faster than control branches prior to adoption. This pattern suggests the observed improvement may reflect pre-existing differences between branches rather than a true effect of the new procedure. **We do not recommend a system-wide rollout based on this evidence alone.**

---

## **FINAL VERDICT**

### **❌ DO NOT APPROVE SYSTEM-WIDE ROLLOUT**

**Confidence Level: LOW**

The evidence is insufficient to support a cooperative-wide implementation. The apparent 2-point improvement cannot be attributed to the new procedure with confidence because:

1. **Selection bias is present**: Branches that adopted the new procedure were already improving faster before adoption
2. **Parallel trends assumption is violated**: This invalidates the standard causal analysis
3. **Alternative explanations are equally plausible**: The improvement may reflect branch motivation, management quality, or other unmeasured factors—not the procedure itself

**If the Board chooses to proceed despite this uncertainty, we recommend:**
- A randomized controlled trial with remaining branches, OR
- A limited pilot (10–20 branches) with rigorous monitoring before any broader expansion

---

## **KEY FINDINGS**

### **Critical Issue: Pre-Existing Trends**

The fundamental assumption for causal inference is that treatment and control groups were on parallel trajectories before the intervention. **This assumption is violated in our data.**

| Period | Treatment-Control Gap | Change |
|--------|----------------------|--------|
| Week 6 (start) | 0.45 points | — |
| Week 14 (pre-switch) | 2.68 points | +2.23 points |
| Week 22 (end) | 4.36 points | +1.68 points |

**The gap widened by 2.23 points BEFORE the switch, but only 1.68 points AFTER.** The rate of improvement actually slowed after implementation (0.28 points/week before vs. 0.21 points/week after).

### **What This Means**

Branches that chose to adopt the new procedure were already on a stronger improvement trajectory. Possible explanations:

1. **Selection bias**: More motivated or better-resourced branches opted into the new procedure
2. **Mean reversion**: Branches that had been underperforming were already recovering
3. **Unobserved factors**: Something else (training, management changes, etc.) drove both the decision to switch AND the score improvements

### **Statistical Estimate (With Caveats)**

The standard difference-in-differences model estimates a 2.07-point effect (95% CI: 1.53 to 2.61, p < 0.001). **However, given the violated assumptions, this estimate is likely biased and should not be interpreted as a causal effect.**

### **Study Limitations**

- Non-random assignment: Branches self-selected into the new procedure
- Only 17 weeks of data; longer follow-up may clarify trends
- No baseline equivalence between treatment and control groups

---

## **NEXT STEPS**

1. **Do NOT proceed with system-wide rollout** until we have stronger evidence of effectiveness

2. **Conduct a randomized trial**: Randomly assign remaining branches to new vs. old procedure to eliminate selection bias

3. **Gather qualitative data**: Interview branch managers to understand why some branches adopted the new procedure and whether other changes coincided with adoption

4. **Extend monitoring**: Continue tracking all branches for additional weeks to see if trends converge or diverge

5. **Consider a pilot expansion**: If proceeding despite uncertainty, expand to a small number of additional branches with careful monitoring before full rollout

---

## **RECOMMENDATION**

**Pause the rollout and gather better evidence.** The current data is consistent with the new procedure having no effect, a small positive effect, or even a negative effect—the pre-existing trend makes it impossible to distinguish. A randomized evaluation or natural experiment with better identification would provide the evidence needed for a confident decision.

---

*Technical note: Difference-in-differences estimation requires parallel pre-trends between treatment and control groups. Event study analysis reveals significant pre-trend differences (p < 0.05 for multiple pre-period weeks), invalidating the causal interpretation of the DiD estimate.*
