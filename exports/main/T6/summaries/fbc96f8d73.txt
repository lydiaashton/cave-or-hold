# **REVISED MEMORANDUM**

**To:** Library Cooperative Board  
**From:** Data Analysis Team  
**Date:** November 2026  
**Subject:** Evaluation of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently conclude the new procedure caused the improvement.** While branches that switched show higher service scores after the change, they were already improving relative to other branches *before* the switch occurred. The evidence is **inconclusive** about whether the procedure itself drives the improvement. We recommend **against a full rollout** until further evidence is gathered.

---

## **Key Findings**

### The Core Problem: Pre-Existing Trends

| Period | Treatment vs. Control Gap | Change |
|--------|--------------------------|--------|
| Week 6 (start) | −1.12 points | — |
| Week 14 (before switch) | +1.75 points | **+2.88 points** |
| Week 22 (end) | +2.71 points | **+0.96 points** |

**Critical observation:** The gap between treatment and control branches widened by **2.88 points BEFORE the switch** but only **0.96 points AFTER**. This suggests branches that chose to switch were already on an improvement trajectory independent of the procedure change.

### Standard Analysis (Potentially Misleading)

| Measure | Value |
|---------|-------|
| Difference-in-Differences estimate | +1.93 points |
| Statistical significance | p < 0.001 |

⚠️ **This estimate assumes parallel trends** (that treatment and control would have followed similar paths without the intervention). **This assumption is violated** by the pre-existing upward trend in the treatment group.

### What the Data Actually Shows

- Treatment branches went from **1.12 points BELOW** control (week 6) to **1.75 points ABOVE** control (week 14) — all before the switch
- After the switch, the gap continued widening but at a **slower rate** (0.12 points/week after vs. 0.36 points/week before)
- This pattern suggests **selection bias**: branches that opted into the new procedure may have had more motivated management, better resources, or other unobserved advantages

---

## **Recommendation**

**Do NOT proceed with full rollout based on current evidence.** The observed association between the new procedure and higher scores cannot be distinguished from pre-existing differences between branches that chose to switch versus those that did not.

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Pause full rollout** – Implementing across all branches based on this evidence risks investing in a procedure that may not deliver the expected benefits.

2. **Run a randomized trial** – Randomly assign a subset of remaining branches to adopt the procedure while others serve as controls. This would establish credible causal evidence.

3. **Investigate selection factors** – Analyze what distinguished branches that chose to switch (size, location, management, baseline performance, etc.) to understand the source of the pre-existing trend.

4. **Gather qualitative evidence** – Interview staff at switched branches to understand whether they perceive the procedure as beneficial and why.

5. **Re-evaluate in 8–12 weeks** – If a randomized trial is conducted, reassess the evidence before making a rollout decision.

---

## **Why This Matters**

A false positive conclusion could lead to:
- Unnecessary implementation costs across 284+ branches
- Staff time spent on training for a procedure with no real benefit
- Opportunity costs (resources not spent on interventions that actually work)

A cautious approach protects the cooperative from investing in changes that appear effective but are not.
