## **The Bottom Line Needs Revision**

**The evidence is mixed, not definitive.**

### **The Problem: Parallel Trends Violation**

The switched branches were **already improving faster** than control branches *before* the procedure change:

| Period | Gap (Switched − Control) |
|--------|-------------------------|
| Week 6 | 0.38 points |
| Week 14 (last pre-switch week) | 2.31 points |
| **Pre-switch gap growth** | **+1.93 points** |

The gap widened by nearly 2 points during the pre-period alone. This means:

1. **The standard DiD estimate (2.0 points) is likely overstated** — it captures pre-existing trends, not just the procedure effect

2. **The immediate jump at implementation was only 0.22 points** (Week 14: 2.31 → Week 15: 2.53)

3. **Switched branches may have been selected because they were already improving** — higher-performing branches may have been chosen for early adoption

---

## **Revised Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently conclude the new procedure caused the improvement.** While switched branches have higher service scores post-implementation, the evidence suggests much of this difference reflects pre-existing trends rather than the procedure itself. The immediate effect at implementation was small (~0.2 points), and the parallel trends assumption required for causal inference is violated.

**Recommendation: Proceed with caution.** Consider a randomized rollout or additional pilot testing before system-wide implementation.

---

## **Revised Next Steps**

1. **Pause system-wide rollout** until we have stronger causal evidence

2. **Randomize the remaining 284 branches** into treatment and control to get a cleaner estimate

3. **Investigate selection criteria** — why were certain branches chosen for early adoption?

4. **Gather qualitative evidence** from switched branches about operational changes and staff feedback

5. **Extend the observation period** — if the post-switch trend accelerates relative to pre-switch, that would strengthen the case
