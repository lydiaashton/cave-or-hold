## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot conclude the new procedure improved service scores.** While branches that switched showed higher scores after week 15, those same branches were already on an upward trajectory *before* the switch. When we account for this pre-existing trend, the estimated effect of the new procedure is actually **slightly negative (-0.6 points)** rather than positive. **We do not recommend system-wide rollout based on this evidence.**

---

## **What Changed in Our Analysis**

Our initial analysis used a standard difference-in-differences approach, which assumes treatment and control branches would have followed parallel trends absent the intervention. **This assumption does not hold.**

### **The Critical Issue: Pre-Treatment Trends**

| Period | Gap Between Groups (Treatment - Control) |
|--------|------------------------------------------|
| Week 6 | -0.35 points |
| Week 10 | +0.81 points |
| Week 14 (just before switch) | +1.51 points |
| Week 15 (just after switch) | +1.65 points |
| Week 22 | +3.62 points |

**The gap was already widening before the switch occurred.** The treatment group was gaining ~0.28 points per week on the control group even before adopting the new procedure.

### **Revised Estimate**

When we extrapolate the pre-treatment trend forward and compare it to actual post-treatment performance:

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Predicted gap (weeks 15-22, based on pre-trend) | 3.30 points |
| Actual gap (weeks 15-22) | 2.70 points |
| **Adjusted treatment effect** | **-0.61 points** |

**Interpretation:** Branches that switched actually performed *worse* than their pre-existing trend would have predicted.

---

## **Why the Initial Analysis Was Misleading**

1. **Non-random assignment:** Branches that switched may have been selected (or self-selected) based on factors related to improving performance
2. **Violated parallel trends:** The treatment group was already improving relative to control before the intervention
3. **Confounding factors:** Something else—management attention, additional resources, seasonal patterns—may explain both the decision to switch and the score improvements

---

## **Recommendation**

**Do not proceed with system-wide rollout based on current evidence.** The data does not support a causal claim that the new procedure improves service scores.

---

## **Next Steps**

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
| **Pause rollout plans** | Evidence does not support effectiveness |
| **Investigate selection process** | Understand why certain branches switched and whether they differed systematically |
| **Consider randomized trial** | If we want causal evidence, randomly assign branches to treatment/control |
| **Gather qualitative feedback** | Interview branch managers about their experience with the new procedure |
| **Collect additional data** | Extend the observation period to see if effects emerge over time |

---

## **What We Can Say With Confidence**

- Branches that switched to the new procedure *did* have higher service scores after week 15
- However, these branches were already outperforming their control counterparts before the switch
- We cannot attribute the improvement to the new procedure itself
- The new procedure may still be beneficial, but this data cannot prove it

---

**We apologize for the initial conclusion.** Proper causal inference requires verifying the parallel trends assumption, which we should have done before making recommendations. We are committed to providing the board with rigorous, defensible analysis.
