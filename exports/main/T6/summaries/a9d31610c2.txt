## **Key Concern: Selection Bias**

The fundamental problem is that branches were **not randomly assigned** to switch procedures. The data reveals:

- **Before the switch** (weeks 6-14), switching branches' scores were already improving relative to non-switching branches by **~0.3 points per week**
- **After the switch** (weeks 15-22), this trend continued at a similar rate
- There is **no clear break** in the pattern at week 15 that would indicate the procedure caused the change

This suggests branches that chose to switch may have been:
- Better-managed overall
- More proactive about service quality
- Facing different local conditions

Switching branches clearly outperformed after week 15. The question is **why** — the procedure, or pre-existing differences between these branches?

---

## **Recommendations**

### **1. Do NOT proceed with full rollout yet**

The current evidence is insufficient to justify the cost and disruption of system-wide implementation.

### **2. Run a randomized trial**

Randomly assign 50-100 branches to the new procedure. This eliminates selection bias and provides a clean causal estimate.

### **3. If proceeding anyway, use phased rollout with evaluation**

- Implement in waves
- Track outcomes carefully
- Be prepared to stop if effects don't materialize

### **4. Investigate why switching branches were different**

Understanding what distinguished early adopters may reveal whether the procedure itself matters, or whether it's about branch characteristics.

---

## **Conclusion**

We cannot confirm the new procedure improved service scores. The observed association likely reflects **which branches chose to switch**, not the procedure's effect. Additional evidence is needed before recommending system-wide adoption.
