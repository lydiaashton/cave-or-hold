**SUBJECT:** Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure  

---

## **Bottom Line**

**Recommendation: Do NOT proceed with system-wide rollout at this time. Gather more evidence first.**

While switched branches showed higher service scores after the procedure change, **we cannot confidently attribute this improvement to the new procedure itself**. The data has a critical flaw: branches that switched were already on different performance trajectories before the change. This undermines our ability to claim the procedure caused the improvement.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **What the Data Shows**

| Group | Before Week 15 | After Week 15 | Change |
|-------|---------------|---------------|--------|
| Switched branches (425) | 51.59 | 52.70 | +1.11 |
| Control branches (284) | 51.24 | 50.40 | -0.84 |
| **Difference-in-Differences** | | | **+1.94** |

The raw numbers look favorable. However...

### **Critical Methodological Concern**

⚠️ **The parallel trends assumption is violated (p < 0.001)**

Difference-in-differences analysis requires that treatment and control groups follow similar trends before the intervention. Our testing shows they did not:

- Switched branches were already improving relative to control branches **before** Week 15
- This pre-existing trend could explain most or all of the observed "effect"
- We cannot distinguish procedure impact from pre-existing momentum

### **Event Study Evidence**

Looking at week-by-week effects:
- **Pre-switch (Weeks 6-14):** Treatment group was steadily gaining on control group
- **Post-switch (Weeks 15-22):** The gap continued widening, but **none of the individual post-switch weeks show statistically significant effects** until Week 22
- This pattern suggests continuation of pre-existing trends, not a discrete treatment effect

### **Effect Size Context**

Even if the full 1.94-point effect is real:
- Represents only 0.20 standard deviations (modest)
- Approximately 3.8% improvement on the service index
- Must be weighed against implementation costs and disruption

---

## **Next Steps**

| Action | Timeline | Rationale |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| **1. Pause rollout decision** | Immediate | Avoid committing resources to unproven intervention |
| **2. Collect additional data** | 8-12 more weeks | Better establish post-switch trends |
| **3. Investigate selection bias** | 2-4 weeks | Understand why certain branches switched and whether they differed systematically |
| **4. Consider randomized pilot** | Next quarter | If feasible, randomly assign remaining branches to treatment/control for cleaner causal inference |
| **5. Qualitative follow-up** | 4-6 weeks | Interview branch managers about implementation quality and other concurrent changes |

### **Questions to Answer Before Rollout**

1. Why did 425 branches switch while 284 did not? Was this voluntary? If so, more motivated branches may have self-selected into treatment.

2. Were there other changes coinciding with Week 15 (seasonal patterns, staffing changes, policy updates)?

3. What is the cost of full implementation (training, disruption, materials)?

4. Could the observed improvement be sustained, or might it fade over time?

---

## **Conclusion**

The evidence is **suggestive but not conclusive**. The new procedure may improve service scores, but our current data cannot confirm this with confidence. Given the methodological limitations and the costs of system-wide change, we recommend **gathering stronger evidence before committing to full rollout**.

A more rigorous evaluation now will prevent potentially costly mistakes and ensure that any eventual rollout is based on solid evidence of effectiveness.
