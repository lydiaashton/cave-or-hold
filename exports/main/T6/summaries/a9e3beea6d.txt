## **BOTTOM LINE**

**We cannot confidently conclude the new procedure caused the improvement.** While switched branches did show higher service scores after the change, our analysis reveals that these branches were **already improving before they switched**. This suggests the observed improvement may reflect **which branches chose to adopt the procedure** rather than the procedure's actual effect. **We recommend against a full rollout until we can obtain more reliable evidence.**

---

## **KEY FINDINGS**

### 1. Initial Results Were Misleading
The simple before/after comparison showed switched branches gaining +2 points. However, this analysis **did not account for pre-existing differences** between branches that chose to switch versus those that didn't.

### 2. Parallel Trends Assumption Is Violated
For a valid causal comparison, treatment and control branches should follow similar trends before the intervention. **This was not the case:**
- Treatment branches were already improving relative to control branches **before** the switch
- 7 out of 9 pre-switch weeks showed statistically significant differences
- Statistical test confirms pre-trends are highly significant (p < 0.0001)

### 3. When Pre-Trends Are Accounted For, the Effect Disappears
| Model | Estimated Effect | Statistical Significance |
|-------|------------------|--------------------------|
| Simple DiD (ignores pre-trends) | +1.98 points | Significant (p < 0.001) |
| DiD **with pre-trend control** | -0.46 points | **Not significant (p = 0.28)** |

### 4. Likely Explanation: Selection Bias
Branches that volunteered to switch may have been:
- Better managed overall
- More motivated to improve
- Already implementing other improvements
- Different in unobserved ways that affect service scores

---

## **RECOMMENDATION**

**Do NOT proceed with system-wide rollout at this time.** The current evidence does not support a causal claim that the new procedure improves service scores.

---

## **What Changed From the Initial Analysis**

The initial summary reported a statistically significant +2 point improvement. However, that analysis relied on the **parallel trends assumption**—that treatment and control branches would have followed similar trajectories absent the intervention. The event study analysis I subsequently conducted shows this assumption does not hold. When we properly account for pre-existing trends, the apparent treatment effect vanishes.

**This is a fundamental limitation of the data, not a statistical error.** Without random assignment or a valid natural experiment, we cannot separate the procedure's effect from the characteristics of branches that chose to adopt it.
