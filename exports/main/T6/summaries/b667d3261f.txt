## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot conclude that the new procedure improved service scores.** 

While branches that switched showed higher scores after the change, they were **already improving faster than other branches before they switched**. This pre-existing trend fully explains the observed "improvement." The analysis cannot separate the effect of the procedure from the fact that higher-performing, faster-improving branches chose to adopt it.

**Recommendation:** Do **not** proceed with system-wide rollout based on this evidence. If the board wishes to evaluate the procedure, conduct a **randomized trial** where branches are randomly assigned to adopt (or not adopt) the new procedure.

---

## **Key Findings**

### 1. The Apparent Effect Is Not Credible

| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Switched Branches | 51.8 | 55.6 | +3.8 |
| Non-Switched Branches | 50.4 | 52.5 | +2.0 |
| **Observed "Effect"** | — | — | **+1.7** |

However, this 1.7-point difference **cannot be attributed to the procedure** because of pre-existing trends.

### 2. Parallel Trends Assumption Is Violated

For a valid comparison, switched and non-switched branches should have been on similar trajectories before the switch. They were not:

- **Non-switched branches** were improving at **0.32 points/week** before week 15
- **Switched branches** were improving at **0.59 points/week** before week 15
- **Difference:** 0.27 points/week (p = 0.004, statistically significant)

Over 8 post-switch weeks, this pre-existing trend difference alone would create approximately **2.2 points** of apparent "effect"—more than the entire 1.7 points we observed.

### 3. What This Means

The data are consistent with **three possibilities**:

1. The procedure has **no effect** (the observed improvement is entirely pre-existing trends)
2. The procedure has a **small positive effect** (partially masked by the trend issue)
3. The procedure has a **negative effect** (branches would have done even better without it)

**We cannot distinguish among these possibilities with this data.**

### 4. Self-Selection Is the Core Problem

Branches chose whether to switch. Those that did were:
- Already higher-performing (average 51.8 vs. 50.4 before the switch)
- Already improving faster (0.59 vs. 0.32 points/week)
- Likely more motivated, better-staffed, or better-managed

These unmeasured advantages—not the procedure itself—likely explain the observed outcomes.

## **Next Steps**

### Recommended Path Forward

1. **Do not expand the procedure** based on current evidence
2. **Design a randomized evaluation**: Randomly assign 50-100 branches to adopt the procedure, with the rest as controls
3. **Collect 12-16 weeks of data** post-assignment to measure true causal effects
4. **Make rollout decision** only after randomized evidence is available

### If the Board Wishes to Proceed Anyway

If operational considerations favor expansion despite inconclusive evidence:

1. **Phase the rollout** carefully, treating each phase as a learning opportunity
2. **Track metrics rigorously** with clear success criteria defined in advance
3. **Be prepared to reverse course** if later branches do not show expected improvements
4. **Document implementation quality** to distinguish procedure effects from execution quality

This analysis **cannot determine whether the new procedure improves service scores**. The observed improvements are entirely consistent with pre-existing differences between branches that chose to switch and those that did not.
