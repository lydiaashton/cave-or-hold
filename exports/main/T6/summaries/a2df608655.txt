# **REVISED MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Board of Directors, Library Cooperative  
**FROM:** Data Analysis Office  
**DATE:** November 15, 2026  
**SUBJECT:** Evaluation of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently conclude the new procedure caused the improvement.** While branches that switched show higher service scores, the data reveals they were already improving *before* the switch. This violates a key assumption needed to attribute the change to the new procedure. **We recommend additional analysis before proceeding with system-wide rollout.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### **1. The Core Problem: Pre-Existing Trends**

An event study analysis reveals that treatment branches were already on an upward trajectory **before** adopting the new procedure:

| Period | Treatment vs. Control Gap | Statistically Significant? |
|--------|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Week 6 | -2.88 points | Yes (p < 0.001) |
| Week 8 | -1.78 points | Yes (p = 0.002) |
| Week 11 | -1.46 points | Yes (p = 0.004) |
| Week 13 | -0.35 points | No |
| **Week 14** | **(Reference)** | **—** |
| Week 15 | -0.12 points | No |
| Week 20 | +1.01 points | No (p = 0.06) |
| Week 22 | +0.75 points | No (p = 0.16) |

**Interpretation:** The treatment group was "catching up" to the control group even before the switch. The gap narrowed from -2.88 to -0.35 in the pre-period alone.

### **2. Post-Treatment Effects Are Not Statistically Significant**

After the switch (weeks 15-22), **none** of the weekly treatment effects reach conventional significance levels (p < 0.05):
- Best week (20): p = 0.06
- Second best (21): p = 0.09
- Average post-treatment effect: +0.46 points (not significant)

### **3. Why the Simple DiD Was Misleading**

The basic difference-in-differences estimate (+1.9 points) appears significant, but this method assumes treatment and control groups would have followed **parallel trends** without the intervention. The event study shows this assumption is **violated** — the treatment group was already improving relative to control before the switch.

### **4. What We Can Say**

| Claim | Supported by Data? |
|-------|-------------------|
| Treatment branches have higher scores after week 15 | ✓ Yes |
| The new procedure *caused* this improvement | ✗ **No** — pre-trends confound this |
| Treatment branches were improving before the switch | ✓ Yes |
| Post-treatment effects are statistically distinguishable from zero | ✗ No |

---

## **Recommendation**

**Do not proceed with system-wide rollout based on current evidence.** The data does not support a causal claim that the new procedure improves service scores. The observed improvement may reflect:
- Pre-existing momentum at treatment branches
- Selection bias (branches that chose to switch may have been different)
- Other unobserved factors

---

## **Next Steps**

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
| **Pause rollout decision** | Current evidence is insufficient for causal claims |
| **Extend the observation period** | More post-treatment weeks may clarify the effect |
| **Investigate branch selection** | Understand why certain branches switched — were they already improving? |
| **Consider randomized rollout** | Future changes should be randomly assigned to enable causal inference |
| **Qualitative follow-up** | Interview branch managers about implementation and other concurrent changes |

---

## **Methodological Note**

The initial analysis used a standard difference-in-differences approach, which produced a statistically significant +1.9 point estimate. However, the event study (examining effects week-by-week) reveals this estimate is likely biased by pre-existing trends. This is a well-known limitation of DiD when the parallel trends assumption is violated.
