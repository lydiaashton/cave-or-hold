# **REVISED MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

**Subject:** Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure  
**Recommendation:** **Pause rollout pending further analysis**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently attribute the score improvement to the new procedure.** While switched branches did outperform control branches by 2 points after the switch, **the treatment group was already pulling ahead before the intervention began**. The difference between treatment and control branches grew from essentially zero in week 6 to +2.0 points by week 14—**before any branch switched procedures**. This pattern suggests the observed improvement may reflect pre-existing differences between the groups rather than a causal effect of the new procedure.

---

## **Key Findings**

### What the Data Shows

| Period | Treatment vs. Control Gap |
|--------|--------------------------|
| Week 6 (start) | -0.11 points |
| Week 14 (eve of switch) | +2.00 points |
| Week 22 (end) | +3.63 points |

**The problem:** The treatment group gained 2.0 points on the control group **before the switch even happened**. The post-switch gain (an additional 1.6 points) is smaller than the pre-switch gain.

### Statistical Concern

The **parallel trends assumption**—the foundation of difference-in-differences analysis—**appears violated**. The treatment and control groups were on different trajectories before the intervention:
- Pre-switch trend: Treatment gaining 0.28 points per week on control (p < 0.001)
- If this trend continued unchanged, we'd expect ~2.25 points of additional gain over 8 weeks
- Actual post-switch gain: ~1.6 points

**This suggests the new procedure may not have added any benefit beyond what was already happening.**

### Alternative Explanations

The treatment branches (425) may differ systematically from control branches (284) in ways we cannot observe:
- More motivated management teams volunteered to switch early
- Better-resourced branches were selected for the pilot
- Branches already improving were more likely to adopt change

---

## **What We Still Don't Know**

1. **Is there a true treatment effect?** The data cannot distinguish between:
   - The procedure causing improvement
   - Already-improving branches being more likely to adopt the procedure

2. **Why were treatment branches diverging before the switch?** This needs investigation before scaling.

3. **Could there still be a benefit?** Yes—the procedure might work, but we haven't proven it yet.

---

## **Recommended Next Steps**

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
| **Pause system-wide rollout** | Avoid investing in a procedure that may not deliver expected benefits |
| **Investigate selection process** | Understand how branches were chosen to switch; were they already outperforming? |
| **Consider randomized rollout** | For remaining branches, randomly assign switch timing to enable cleaner causal inference |
| **Qualitative follow-up** | Interview branch managers to understand what else changed during this period |
| **Extended monitoring** | Continue tracking both groups for another 8-12 weeks to see if gaps widen or converge |

---

## **Conclusion**

**We cannot recommend rollout based on this evidence.** The 2-point difference we observed is real, but we cannot confidently say the new procedure caused it. The most honest interpretation is that branches that switched were already on a better trajectory than those that didn't—possibly because of unobserved factors like management quality, resources, or motivation.

Before committing resources to a system-wide rollout, we should:
1. Understand why treatment and control branches differed before the switch
2. Design a more rigorous evaluation (ideally with random assignment)
3. Gather qualitative evidence about what's driving improvements

**The new procedure may still be worthwhile—but we need better evidence before asking the Board to approve a full rollout.**
