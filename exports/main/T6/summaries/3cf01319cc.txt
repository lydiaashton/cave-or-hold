# **REVISED BOARD MEMORANDUM**

---

## **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

---

### **BOTTOM LINE**

**Recommendation: Do NOT proceed with system-wide rollout at this time. Conduct additional evaluation first.**

The apparent 1.8-point service score improvement **cannot be confidently attributed to the new procedure**. Branches that adopted the new procedure were already improving faster than other branches *before* the switch. This selection bias means the true effect is uncertain—it could be smaller than estimated, zero, or even negative.

---

### **KEY FINDINGS**

| Metric | Value | Concern |
|--------|-------|---------|
| **Raw DiD estimate** | +1.81 points | Assumes parallel trends |
| **Pre-period trend** | +0.32 pts/week | Trends were NOT parallel |
| **Pre-period gap change** | -1.1 to +1.8 pts | 2.9-pt swing before switch |
| **Statistical test** | p < 0.001 | Pre-trend is significant |
| **Expected post gap** (if trend continued) | ~3.0 points | Based on extrapolation |
| **Actual post gap** | ~2.1 points | Lower than expected |

---

### **CRITICAL ISSUE: VIOLATED ASSUMPTION**

The difference-in-differences method requires that treatment and control branches would have followed **parallel trends** absent the intervention. This assumption is **clearly violated**:

```
Pre-Period Treatment vs. Control Gap:
Week 6:  -1.1 points (treatment behind)
Week 10: +0.1 points (caught up)
Week 14: +1.8 points (treatment ahead)
```

Branches that chose to switch were already on an upward trajectory. Some portion of the post-switch improvement would have happened anyway.

**More concerning:** If the pre-period trend had continued unchanged, we would have expected a ~3.0-point gap by mid post-period. The observed ~2.1-point gap is actually *lower* than this expectation, suggesting the procedure may have added little or no value beyond existing momentum.

---

### **WHAT WE CAN CONFIDENTLY SAY**

1. Branches using the new procedure have higher service scores post-switch
2. The improvement is statistically significant in a raw comparison
3. **We cannot isolate how much is due to the procedure vs. branch characteristics**

### **WHAT WE CANNOT CONFIDENTLY SAY**

1. The causal impact of the procedure itself
2. Whether non-adopter branches would see similar gains
3. Whether the effect will persist or fade over time

---

### **RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS**

| Action | Rationale | Timeline |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **1. Pause system-wide rollout** | Evidence is insufficient for cooperative-wide commitment | Immediate |
| **2. Randomize remaining branches** | Assign non-adopters randomly to treatment/control to establish causal effect | 2–4 weeks |
| **3. Extend monitoring period** | Determine if effects persist, grow, or fade | 8–12 weeks |
| **4. Interview early adopters** | Understand what else changed alongside the procedure (training, staffing, motivation) | 4 weeks |
| **5. Re-analyze with randomized data** | Produce defensible causal estimate for board decision | After randomization period |

---

### **ALTERNATIVE: CONTROLLED ROLLOUT**

If the board prefers to proceed despite uncertainty:

- Roll out to **50% of remaining branches** (randomly selected)
- Keep the other 50% as a control group
- Monitor for 8–12 weeks before full commitment
- This creates the evidence needed for a confident decision

---

### **CONCLUSION**

The current evidence is **suggestive but not definitive**. The new procedure *may* improve service scores, but the branches that adopted it were already improving. A system-wide rollout risks investing resources in a procedure whose true impact is unknown. A controlled, evidence-based approach will produce the clarity the board needs to make a confident decision.
