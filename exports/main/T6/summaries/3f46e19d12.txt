## **BOTTOM LINE**

**The evidence is inconclusive. Do not proceed with full rollout yet.**

While branches that switched to the new procedure showed higher service scores afterward, **the analysis cannot reliably attribute this improvement to the new procedure**. The switched branches were already on a different trajectory before the switch, which undermines the validity of the comparison. More rigorous evaluation is needed before making a system-wide decision.

---

## **KEY FINDINGS**

### 1. The Critical Problem: Pre-Existing Differences

The difference-in-differences method requires that treatment and control groups follow **parallel trends** before the intervention. **This assumption is violated in our data:**

| Week | Switched Branches | Control Branches | Gap |
|------|------------------|------------------|-----|
| Week 6 | 48.48 | 48.47 | 0.01 |
| Week 10 | 49.61 | 49.21 | 0.40 |
| Week 14 | 47.99 | 46.34 | **1.64** |

**The gap was already widening by 0.27 points per week BEFORE the switch** (p=0.002). Over the 9-week pre-period, the gap grew by 1.63 points—nearly as large as the estimated "treatment effect" of 1.89 points.

### 2. What This Means

The observed improvement may reflect:
- **Selection bias**: Branches that chose to switch may have been systematically different (e.g., more motivated management, better resources, already improving)
- **Pre-existing trends**: The switched branches were already on an upward trajectory
- **The actual procedure effect**: Possibly some real benefit, but we cannot isolate it from the above factors

**We cannot determine how much (if any) of the 1.89-point difference is due to the new procedure itself.**

### 3. What We Can Say

| Finding | Confidence |
|---------|------------|
| Switched branches had higher scores after week 15 | ✓ Confirmed |
| The difference is statistically significant | ✓ Confirmed |
| The new procedure **caused** the improvement | ✗ **Cannot determine** |
| Rollout to all branches will produce similar gains | ✗ **Cannot predict** |

---

## **RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS**

### 1. **Pause Full Rollout**
Do not commit resources to system-wide implementation until the effect is better understood.

### 2. **Gather More Evidence**
- **Extend the observation period**: Collect several more months of data to see if the gap continues to widen or stabilizes
- **Interview switched branches**: Understand implementation experiences, challenges, and perceived benefits
- **Compare similar branches**: Match switched and non-switched branches on observable characteristics (size, staffing, location, baseline performance) and re-analyze

### 3. **Consider a Randomized Rollout**
For the remaining 284 branches, consider **randomly assigning** the rollout order. This would create a cleaner evaluation design where selection bias is eliminated.

### 4. **Monitor for Negative Effects**
Track whether any switched branches experienced disruptions, increased costs, or staff concerns that aren't captured in the service score.

## **Why This Revision Matters**

The original analysis used a standard method (difference-in-differences), but that method's validity depends on an assumption that our data does not satisfy. Presenting the 1.89-point effect as causal would be misleading. The board deserves to know that **branches self-selected into switching**, and those branches were already diverging from controls before the policy changed. This is a common challenge in observational studies, and the appropriate response is caution—not confident rollout recommendations.
