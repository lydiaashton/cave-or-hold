You're right to question this. Let me be more careful about what the data actually shows.

**The statistical evidence is solid:**
- DiD coefficient = 1.84, p = 0.003, 95% CI [0.64, 3.04] — this does not include zero
- The effect persists with controls (1.82, p = 0.002)
- Parallel trends assumption holds (pre-period trends not significantly different)

**However, there are important nuances:**

1. **The effect is modest**: 1.8 points on a ~50-point scale is about 3.7%. Meaningful, but not transformative.

2. **Week-to-week variation in post-period**: Looking at the weekly effects after the switch:
   - Weeks 15-17: +0.70 to +0.81 (positive)
   - Weeks 18-20: -0.52 to -0.20 (negative)
   - Weeks 21-22: +0.26 to +1.04 (mixed/positive)
   
   This suggests the effect may not be stable — there could be seasonal factors or implementation variation.

3. **Control group declined**: The non-switched branches dropped 0.6 points post-period. Part of the DiD effect is that treatment branches *avoided* this decline, not just that they improved.

4. **Only 8 weeks post-data**: We don't know if this effect persists long-term.

**Revised Bottom Line:**

> **The evidence supports rolling out the new procedure, but with measured expectations.** Branches using the new procedure achieved service scores approximately **1.8 points higher** than they would have under the old procedure (p = 0.003). This is a real, statistically reliable effect. However, the improvement is modest, varies week-to-week, and we have only 8 weeks of post-implementation data. We recommend proceeding with the rollout while continuing to monitor outcomes and supporting branches that don't see improvements.
