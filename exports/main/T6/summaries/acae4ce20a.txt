# **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

**Prepared for:** Library Cooperative Board  
**Date:** November 2026  
**Analysis Period:** Weeks 6–22 (17 weeks of data)

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure helped branches maintain service scores, but did not improve them above the control group.** Branches that adopted the new procedure avoided the decline seen in branches that kept the old procedure. The statistical evidence shows the new procedure had a measurable effect (+1.8 points relative to control, p=0.002), but treatment branches still scored lower than control branches both before and after the switch.

**Key nuance:** The benefit is **stability**, not absolute improvement. Treatment branches started ~3 points below control branches and ended ~1 point below - they declined less, but did not surpass the control group.

**Recommendation:** The procedure shows promise for maintaining service quality during change. Proceed with rollout, but set realistic expectations: this helps branches avoid decline, not boost scores above peers.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **Study Design**
- **150 branches** tracked over **17 weeks** (weeks 6–22)
- **90 branches** adopted the new procedure starting week 15; **60 branches** kept the old procedure
- **Important:** Treatment branches had lower baseline scores than control branches (51.2 vs 54.2)

### **What Actually Happened**

| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|--------------|--------------|--------|
| Control (old procedure) | 54.2 | 52.9 | **-1.3** ↓ |
| Treatment (new procedure) | 51.2 | 51.6 | **+0.5** → |

**Estimated effect: +1.8 points relative to control** (95% CI: +0.7 to +2.9; p=0.002)

**But critically:** Treatment branches scored **below** control branches both before (-3.0 points) and after (-1.2 points) the switch.

### **Supporting Evidence**

1. **Parallel trends validated:** Before the switch, both groups showed similar week-to-week patterns, supporting the validity of the comparison.

2. **The effect is real but modest:** The 1.8-point difference is statistically significant (p=0.002) but represents only 3.4% of the baseline score and 0.18 standard deviations.

3. **Treatment branches never led:** In every single week after the switch, control branches outscored treatment branches. The "benefit" is that treatment branches held steady while control branches declined.

4. **Why did control branches decline?** The overall system average was stable (52.4 before, 52.1 after). The control group's decline appears specific to those 60 branches - possibly regression to the mean since they started with unusually high scores.

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Proceed with cautious rollout:** The new procedure shows a real, statistically significant benefit for maintaining service scores. However, frame expectations appropriately - this is about resilience, not breakthrough improvement.

2. **Investigate the control group decline:** Understand why the 60 control branches declined while the system overall was stable. This may reveal confounding factors.

3. **Monitor post-rollout:** Track whether the stability effect replicates when lower-scoring branches (more like the treatment group) adopt the procedure.

4. **Consider branch selection:** The 90 branches that switched started with lower scores. They may have been selected *because* they were underperforming. Future rollouts should account for baseline performance.

5. **Cost-benefit analysis:** Before full rollout, assess whether the operational cost of the new procedure justifies a ~1.8-point stability benefit.

## **Technical Note**

Analysis used difference-in-differences regression with cluster-robust standard errors (clustered by branch). The parallel trends assumption was confirmed (p=0.69 for pre-trend differences). The treatment effect is statistically significant but should be interpreted as relative stability rather than absolute improvement. The pre-existing gap between treatment and control groups (-3.0 points) suggests non-random selection into the pilot program.
