# **Evaluation of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure did not harm service quality—and likely improved it modestly.** This is reassuring news for the rollout decision, since service score degradation was a potential risk.

However, **the board's rollout decision should be based primarily on whether the new procedure achieved its intended operational goals** (efficiency, cost savings, staff workflow, etc.). The service score improvement is a **favorable secondary finding** that removes one potential objection to rollout, but should not be the primary driver of the decision.

---

## **Key Findings**

### Analysis Overview
- **150 branches** tracked over **17 weeks** (weeks 6–22)
- **90 branches** switched to the new procedure; **60 branches** retained the old procedure
- Switch occurred at **week 15**
- Method: Difference-in-differences comparison with cluster-robust standard errors

### Service Score Results

| Group | Before Week 15 | After Week 15 | Change |
|-------|----------------|---------------|--------|
| Old Procedure (Control) | 49.22 | 47.36 | −1.86 |
| New Procedure (Treatment) | 50.91 | 51.10 | +0.19 |
| **Net Treatment Effect** | | | **+2.12** |

- **Effect size: +2.12 points** (95% CI: 0.86 to 3.38)
- **p-value: 0.001** — highly unlikely to be due to chance
- Result holds after controlling for staffing levels and visitor volume

### What This Means

1. **Unexpected upside**: The procedure change was not intended to improve service scores, yet it did. This suggests the new rotation/shelving method may have secondary benefits (e.g., easier item location, cleaner shelves, more staff time for patron assistance).

2. **Protective effect**: Control branch scores declined after week 15 while treatment branches held steady. The new procedure may buffer against factors that typically depress service quality.

3. **No trade-off**: Branches gained service score improvements without sacrificing the operational benefits the new procedure was designed to deliver.

4. **Modest magnitude**: +2.1 points on a ~50-point scale is approximately 4%. Statistically significant, but the board should judge whether this is operationally meaningful for your service standards.

---

## **Final Verdict**

**From a service quality perspective: Proceed with rollout.**

The evidence shows the new procedure has **no detectable downside** for service scores and a **statistically significant upside**. This removes service quality as a potential objection to the rollout decision.

**However**, the board should make the final decision based on:
- Whether the new procedure achieved its **intended operational objectives** (rotation efficiency, shelving workflow, cost implications)
- Whether the **~4% service score improvement** is meaningful for your cooperative's performance targets
- Any **qualitative feedback** from the 90 early-adopter branches about implementation experience

If the operational case is neutral or positive, this analysis provides additional confidence that rollout will not harm—and may modestly improve—patron-facing service quality.

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Review operational metrics** from the 90 early-adopter branches to confirm the procedure achieved its primary goals

2. **If proceeding with rollout**: Track service scores for 8–12 weeks post-implementation to confirm effects persist at scale

3. **Investigate the mechanism**: Since this improvement was unexpected, consider qualitative follow-up (staff surveys, patron feedback) to understand *why* the new procedure affected service scores. This could reveal additional optimization opportunities.

4. **Capture implementation lessons** from the 90 early-adopter branches to streamline training and reduce transition friction

---

*Analysis based on 2,550 weekly observations. Standard errors clustered by branch. Prepared for Board Review.*
