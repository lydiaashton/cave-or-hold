# Board Memorandum

**To:** Board of Directors, Library Cooperative  
**From:** Data Analysis Team  
**Date:** November 15, 2026  
**Subject:** Evaluation of New Branch Shelving & Stock Rotation Procedure

---

## Final Verdict

**DO NOT PROCEED WITH SYSTEM-WIDE ROLLOUT**

The evidence does not support a causal claim that the new procedure improves service scores. The observed improvement is attributable to pre-existing differences between branches that chose to switch and those that did not.

---

## Bottom Line

While branches that switched to the new procedure show higher service scores after the change, **we cannot confidently attribute this improvement to the procedure itself.** The branches that chose to switch were already improving faster than other branches before the change. This pre-existing trend could explain the entire observed effect. We recommend a **randomized controlled trial** before any broader implementation.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Apparent Effect

Branches that switched do show higher scores after the change:

| Period | Switched Branches | Control Branches | Gap |
|--------|------------------|------------------|-----|
| Before switch (weeks 6–14) | 50.3 | 48.7 | 1.6 points |
| After switch (weeks 15–22) | 55.6 | 52.0 | 3.6 points |
| **Gap increase** | — | — | **2.0 points** |

### 2. Critical Problem: Violated Assumption

The difference-in-differences method requires that treatment and control groups were on **parallel trends** before the intervention. **This assumption is violated:**

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Control group pre-switch trend | +0.78 points/week |
| Treatment group pre-switch trend | +1.04 points/week |
| **Difference in trends** | **+0.25 points/week (p = 0.0002)** |

The treatment group was already improving **0.25 points/week faster** than the control group before the procedure change.

### 3. What This Means

Over 8 post-switch weeks, the pre-existing trend differential alone could explain:

> **0.25 points/week × 8 weeks = 2.04 points**

This essentially equals the entire observed effect of 2.02 points. **The bias-adjusted treatment effect is approximately zero.**

### 4. Likely Explanation

Branches that volunteered to switch may have had:
- More motivated or better-trained staff
- Better management willing to try new procedures
- More resources to invest in improvement
- Existing momentum on service quality initiatives

These factors—not the procedure itself—may explain the observed improvement.

---

## Limitations

| Issue | Severity | Impact |
|-------|----------|--------|
| Non-random selection into treatment | **High** | Cannot isolate procedure effect |
| Parallel trends assumption violated | **High** | DiD estimate likely biased upward |
| Only 8 weeks post-switch data | Medium | Limited ability to assess sustainability |
| No cost data available | Medium | Cannot assess cost-effectiveness |

---

## Recommended Next Steps

| Priority | Action | Rationale |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| 1 | **Pause system-wide rollout** | Current evidence does not support causal claim |
| 2 | **Randomize remaining branches** | Assign 284 control branches randomly to treatment or continued control |
| 3 | **Collect 12+ weeks of post-randomization data** | Establish reliable causal estimate |
| 4 | **Track implementation costs** | Enable cost-benefit analysis |
| 5 | **Interview switched branches** | Understand qualitative factors behind their improvement |

---

## Conclusion

The observed association between the new procedure and higher service scores **cannot be interpreted as causal** given the violated parallel trends assumption. The branches that switched were already on an upward trajectory, and this pre-existing trend likely explains the observed improvement.

**We recommend against system-wide rollout until a randomized evaluation provides clearer evidence of the procedure's true effect.**

---

*This analysis reflects a rigorous assessment of the causal identification strategy. The original observational comparison did not adequately account for pre-existing differences in trends between groups.*
