# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

## **Evaluation of New Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

---

### **Bottom Line**

**Proceed with rollout, but with tempered expectations.** The new procedure produced a **statistically significant benefit**, but not in the way we might have hoped. Treatment branches did not substantially improve; instead, they **maintained their service scores** while branches using the old procedure declined. The new procedure appears to provide **protection against deterioration** rather than active improvement.

---

### **Key Findings**

| Metric | Treatment Branches | Control Branches |
|--------|-------------------|------------------|
| Before switch | 50.30 | 48.88 |
| After switch | 50.56 | 46.88 |
| **Change** | **+0.26** | **−2.00** |

**Net treatment effect: +2.20 points** (95% CI: 0.89 to 3.51; p = 0.001)

**What this means:**
- The new procedure **prevented decline** during a period when service scores fell system-wide
- Treatment branches essentially **held their ground** while others slipped
- This is a **defensive benefit**—valuable, but different from active improvement

**Why we're confident the effect is real:**
- Statistically significant at p = 0.001
- Groups were comparable at baseline (p = 0.30 for pre-switch difference)
- Parallel trends confirmed before the switch
- Effect persists after controlling for staffing and visitor volume

---

### **Important Context**

We expected no difference. The data show a real effect, but it's modest:
- Only **51% of treatment branches** showed individual improvement
- The average treatment branch improved by less than 1 point
- The benefit is primarily **avoiding the 2-point decline** that affected control branches

**Possible explanations for the control group decline:**
- Seasonal factors (later weeks of the year)
- System-wide pressures affecting all branches
- Something specific to branches that chose not to switch

If the decline is due to system-wide factors, the new procedure offers **meaningful protection**. If it's specific to control branches, the benefit may not generalize.

---

### **Final Verdict**

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **Did the procedure change service scores?** | Yes, statistically significant (p = 0.001) |
| **Is the effect large?** | No—approximately 2 points on a ~50-point scale |
| **Did scores actually improve?** | Barely (+0.26 points). The effect is mostly *preventing decline* |
| **Should we roll it out?** | **Yes, with caveats** |

**Reasoning:**
1. The effect is **real and statistically reliable**—not a false positive
2. The benefit is **defensive resilience**, not transformative improvement
3. There is **no evidence of harm**—scores did not decline in treatment branches
4. Given we expected *no effect*, finding *any protective benefit* is a positive surprise
5. The rollout decision should weigh the **modest benefit against implementation costs**

**Bottom-line recommendation:** **Approve the rollout**, but communicate to stakeholders that the new procedure is a **stabilizing measure**, not a performance booster. Expect service scores to hold steady, not rise.

### **Technical Summary**

- **Method:** Difference-in-differences with cluster-robust standard errors
- **Sample:** 150 branches, 2,550 branch-week observations
- **Effect size:** +2.20 points (4.4% of baseline)
- **Confidence:** 95% CI [0.89, 3.51]

---

*The effect is real and statistically significant. But contrary to a simple "improvement" story, the new procedure's main benefit appears to be resilience—keeping scores stable when they otherwise would have fallen.*
