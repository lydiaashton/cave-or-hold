# **Board Summary: Impact of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure on Service Scores**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The evidence is inconclusive.** While a simple before/after comparison suggests the new procedure improved service scores, a more rigorous analysis reveals that branches assigned to switch were already on different trajectories than control branches *before* the change occurred. This undermines confidence that the observed improvement was caused by the new procedure rather than pre-existing differences between the groups.

**Recommendation: Do not proceed with system-wide rollout based on this evidence alone. Gather additional data or consider a randomized pilot.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### What the Simple Analysis Shows
- Switched branches improved by 3.84 points; control branches improved by 1.35 points
- Simple difference-in-differences estimate: **+2.33 points** (p = 0.0006)
- This initially appeared statistically significant

### What Deeper Analysis Reveals

| Period | Statistically Significant Coefficients |
|--------|----------------------------------------|
| **Before switch** (weeks -9 to -2) | **5 of 8 weeks** show significant differences |
| **After switch** (weeks 0 to 7) | **1 of 8 weeks** shows significant effect |

**This pattern is problematic.** If the new procedure caused the improvement, we would expect:
- No significant differences *before* the switch (parallel trends)
- Consistent significant effects *after* the switch

Instead, we observe the opposite: significant pre-existing differences and inconsistent post-switch effects.

### Why This Matters

The analysis method (difference-in-differences) relies on the assumption that treated and control branches would have followed similar trends absent the intervention. **This assumption appears violated.** The 2.33-point "effect" may reflect:
- Pre-existing differences between branch groups, not the procedure itself
- Different underlying trajectories unrelated to the switch
- Non-random assignment of which branches switched

---

## **Next Steps**

### Recommended Actions
1. **Pause system-wide rollout** until stronger evidence is available
2. **Investigate why branches were assigned** to switch vs. control—was it random or based on characteristics that also affect service scores?
3. **Consider a randomized controlled trial** with proper random assignment of branches to new vs. old procedure
4. **Collect additional data** on branch characteristics that might explain the pre-trend differences

### If Proceeding Anyway
If the board wishes to proceed despite uncertain evidence:
1. **Implement gradually** with careful monitoring
2. **Set clear success metrics** and evaluation timeline
3. **Be prepared to reverse** if improvements don't materialize

---

## **Final Verdict**

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Did service scores improve in switched branches? | **Yes** – but they also improved in control branches |
| Can we attribute this to the new procedure? | **No** – pre-existing differences confound the result |
| Should we roll out to all branches? | **Not yet** – evidence is insufficient to justify the decision |
| Was the initial expectation (no effect) correct? | **Possibly** – we cannot rule this out |

**The cooperative's initial expectation—that the procedure change would make no difference—may yet prove correct.** The apparent improvement in service scores cannot be confidently attributed to the new procedure given the violated assumptions underlying the analysis. **More rigorous evidence is needed before committing to a system-wide rollout.**

---

*Analysis based on branches.csv (2,550 observations, 150 branches, weeks 6–22). Event study methodology reveals pre-trend violations that undermine causal interpretation of difference-in-differences estimates.*
