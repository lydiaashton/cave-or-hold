# **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

## **Bottom Line**

**The evidence is inconclusive.** While branches that switched to the new procedure showed higher service scores after week 15, **the treatment group was already improving faster than the control group BEFORE the switch**. This violates a key assumption of the analysis and means we cannot confidently attribute the improvement to the new procedure itself.

**Recommendation:** Do **not** proceed with system-wide rollout until further evidence is gathered.

---

## **Key Findings**

### 1. The Core Problem: Pre-Existing Trends
- Treatment branches were improving by **0.32 points/week MORE** than control branches even BEFORE the switch (p < 0.001)
- Over the 8-week post-switch period, this pre-existing trend alone would explain **~2.6 points** of divergence
- The observed treatment effect is only **1.8 points** — meaning the entire effect could be due to pre-existing trends, not the procedure change

### 2. Visual Evidence of Early Divergence
| Period | Treatment-Control Difference |
|--------|------------------------------|
| Weeks 6–11 | 0.05 points (no meaningful gap) |
| Weeks 12–14 | 1.57 points (divergence begins **before** switch) |
| Weeks 15–22 | 2.42 points (gap persists after switch) |

The gap opened up in weeks 12–14, **before** the official switch at week 15.

### 3. What This Means
The branches that volunteered (or were selected) to switch may have been:
- **Already improving** due to other factors (better management, more resources, etc.)
- **Early adopters** who started the new procedure before week 15
- **Systematically different** from branches that kept the old procedure

Any of these would invalidate the comparison.

### 4. What the Analysis Does Show
- Service scores **did** increase more in switching branches after week 15
- The statistical association is real (p < 0.001)
- **But correlation is not causation** — we cannot isolate the procedure's effect from pre-existing differences

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Pause the rollout** — The current evidence does not support system-wide adoption

2. **Investigate the early divergence** — Why did treatment branches start pulling ahead in weeks 12–14?
   - Was there early adoption?
   - Were switching branches systematically different?
   - Were there other concurrent changes?

3. **Consider a randomized trial** — For future changes, randomly assign branches to treatment/control to ensure comparability

4. **Gather qualitative data** — Interview branch managers to understand what else may have changed alongside the procedure

5. **Re-evaluate in 3–6 months** — With more data and better understanding of the confounding factors, reassess whether the procedure itself drives improvement

---

## **Final Verdict**

**NOT READY FOR ROLLOUT**

The data shows an association between the new procedure and higher service scores, but we cannot determine whether the procedure **caused** the improvement. The branches that switched were already on a stronger upward trajectory before the change occurred. Rolling out the procedure system-wide based on this evidence would risk investing resources in a change that may not deliver the expected benefits. Further investigation is required before making a go/no-go decision.

---

*Analysis based on difference-in-differences regression with cluster-robust standard errors. The parallel trends assumption — critical for causal inference — is violated in this data.*
