# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

**Subject:** Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure  
**Date:** Analysis of weeks 6–22, 709 branches  
**Prepared by:** Data Analytics Team

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently attribute the score improvement to the new procedure.** While switched branches did outperform control branches by 2 points after the switch, **the treatment group was already pulling ahead before the intervention began**. The difference between treatment and control branches grew from essentially zero in week 6 to +2.0 points by week 14—**before any branch switched procedures**.

---

## **Final Verdict**

### **DO NOT PROCEED WITH SYSTEM-WIDE ROLLOUT**

**Confidence Level:** Low  
**Evidence Quality:** Insufficient for causal inference  
**Recommendation:** Pause and redesign evaluation

**Rationale:** The fundamental assumption required to claim the procedure caused the improvement—that treatment and control branches would have followed parallel trends absent the intervention—is violated by the data. The treatment group was already gaining 0.28 points per week on the control group before the switch occurred. This pattern is consistent with **selection bias** (better-performing or more motivated branches self-selecting into the pilot) rather than a true treatment effect.

**Risk of Proceeding:** Rolling out a procedure that may not deliver the expected 2-point improvement would consume resources without guaranteed benefit, and could displace other interventions that might be more effective.

---

## **Key Findings**

### What the Data Shows

| Period | Treatment vs. Control Gap |
|--------|--------------------------|
| Week 6 (start) | -0.11 points |
| Week 14 (eve of switch) | +2.00 points |
| Week 22 (end) | +3.63 points |

**The problem:** The treatment group gained 2.0 points on the control group **before the switch even happened**. The post-switch gain (an additional 1.6 points) is smaller than the pre-switch gain.

### Statistical Concern

The **parallel trends assumption**—the foundation of difference-in-differences analysis—**is violated**:
- Pre-switch trend: Treatment gaining 0.28 points per week on control (p < 0.001)
- If this trend continued unchanged, we'd expect ~2.25 points of additional gain over 8 weeks
- Actual post-switch gain: ~1.6 points

**This suggests the new procedure may not have added any benefit beyond what was already happening.**

### Alternative Explanations

The treatment branches (425) may differ systematically from control branches (284) in ways we cannot observe:
- More motivated management teams volunteered to switch early
- Better-resourced branches were selected for the pilot
- Branches already improving were more likely to adopt change

---

## **What We Still Don't Know**

1. **Is there a true treatment effect?** The data cannot distinguish between:
   - The procedure causing improvement
   - Already-improving branches being more likely to adopt the procedure

2. **Why were treatment branches diverging before the switch?** This needs investigation before scaling.

3. **Could there still be a benefit?** Yes—the procedure might work, but we haven't proven it yet.

---

---

## **Path Forward**

To obtain credible evidence on whether the procedure works:

1. **Randomized rollout:** For the remaining 284 control branches (and any treatment branches willing to revert), randomly assign switch timing. This eliminates selection bias.

2. **Staggered implementation:** Roll out in waves with random assignment, allowing each wave to serve as a control for the next.

3. **Qualitative investigation:** Understand what else changed in treatment branches during the study period—new management? Additional training? Other concurrent initiatives?

---

## **Conclusion**

**The new procedure may still be worthwhile—but we need better evidence before asking the Board to approve a full rollout.** The current analysis cannot distinguish between a genuine treatment effect and pre-existing differences between branches that chose to switch versus those that didn't.

We recommend investing in a more rigorous evaluation now rather than risking a costly system-wide rollout that may not deliver expected benefits.
