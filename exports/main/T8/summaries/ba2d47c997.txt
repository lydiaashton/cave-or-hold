# **Board Summary: Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot conclude that the new procedure improved service scores.** 

While branches that switched show higher scores after the change, they were **already improving faster than other branches before the switch**. The gap between switched and non-switched branches widened at nearly the **identical rate** before and after the procedure change (0.30 vs. 0.31 points per week).

The apparent 2-point "effect" is almost entirely explained by pre-existing differences between branches, not the procedure itself.

**Recommendation:** Do **not** proceed with a full rollout based on this evidence.

---

## **Final Verdict**

> **The data does not support rolling out the new procedure to all branches.**
>
> This study cannot answer whether the procedure works—only that branches chosen to adopt it early were already outperforming. Before investing cooperative-wide resources in this change, the board should require **proper experimental evidence** from a randomized trial or a more rigorous quasi-experimental design.
>
> **Vote recommendation: Deferral pending better evidence.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### 1. The Critical Problem: Violated Assumption
The standard analysis method (difference-in-differences) requires that treatment and control groups would have followed **parallel trends** without the intervention. This assumption is **clearly violated**:

| Period | Gap Growth Rate |
|--------|-----------------|
| Before switch (weeks 6-14) | +0.30 points/week |
| After switch (weeks 15-22) | +0.31 points/week |

The gap widened at the **same rate** before and after. This suggests the procedure itself had **little to no additional effect**.

### 2. Gap Trajectory
| Week | Gap (Switched − Non-Switched) |
|------|-------------------------------|
| 6 (first observation) | 0.20 points |
| 14 (last week before switch) | 2.63 points |
| 15 (first week after switch) | 2.74 points |
| 22 (last observation) | 4.91 points |

**There is no visible "jump" or acceleration when the switch occurred.** The gap simply continued its pre-existing upward trajectory.

### 3. What This Means
- Branches selected for early adoption were **already on a better trajectory**
- The 1.96-point "effect" from standard analysis is **not credible** as a causal estimate
- Projecting the pre-switch trend forward predicts a gap of 5.06 points at week 22; the actual was 4.91—**slightly below** what we'd expect without any treatment effect

### 4. Alternative Explanations
Branches that switched may have had:
- More motivated or better-trained staff
- Stronger management
- Better physical facilities
- More engaged local communities
- Other unmeasured advantages

These factors—not the procedure itself—likely drove the observed improvement.

---

## **Next Steps**

### Immediate Actions
1. **Pause the rollout**—do not expand the procedure to additional branches based on this evidence
2. **Communicate findings** to stakeholders who may have expected a different conclusion
3. **Acknowledge the selection bias**—branches were not randomly assigned to switch

### Better Evaluation Approaches
4. **Randomized controlled trial**: Randomly assign remaining branches to treatment or control to eliminate selection bias
5. **Matched comparison**: Pair switched branches with similar non-switched branches based on pre-switch characteristics and trends
6. **Longer observation window**: Collect more data to see if effects emerge or fade over time

### Investigate What Actually Works
7. **Study high-performing branches** (both switched and non-switched) to identify common success factors
8. **Interview branch managers** about implementation conditions and contextual factors
9. **Consider whether the procedure works only under certain conditions** (e.g., adequate staffing, specific branch sizes)

---

## **Methodology Note**

The initial difference-in-differences estimate of +1.94 points is **statistically valid but causally misleading** because the parallel trends assumption is violated. When treatment and control groups have different pre-existing trajectories, the DiD estimator conflates the treatment effect with those pre-existing differences. This is a well-known limitation in quasi-experimental research and requires either randomization or more sophisticated methods (e.g., synthetic control, interrupted time series with trend adjustment) to address.
