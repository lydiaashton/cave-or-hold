# **Board Summary: New Branch Shelving Procedure Evaluation**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The evidence is inconclusive.** While branches that adopted the new procedure showed higher service scores after the switch, this improvement cannot be confidently attributed to the procedure itself. Switched branches were already on a faster improvement trajectory before adopting the new procedure. When accounting for these pre-existing trends, the procedure shows **no statistically significant effect** on service scores.

**Recommendation:** Do not proceed with system-wide rollout yet. Conduct additional evaluation with a more rigorous study design.

---

## **Final Verdict**

> **The new procedure has not been proven to improve service scores.**
>
> The apparent 1.8-point improvement is attributable to pre-existing differences between branches that switched and those that did not—not to the procedure itself. Rolling out the procedure system-wide based on the current evidence would be premature and may not deliver the expected benefits.
>
> **The board should withhold approval for system-wide rollout pending a randomized controlled trial or other rigorous evaluation that can isolate the true causal effect of the procedure.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### 1. Initial Analysis Was Misleading
| Analysis Method | Estimated Effect | 95% CI | P-value |
|-----------------|-----------------|--------|---------|
| Standard comparison | +1.80 points | 1.24 to 2.36 | <0.001 |
| **Adjusted for pre-existing trends** | **-0.70 points** | **-1.56 to +0.16** | **0.11** |

The standard analysis suggested a clear benefit, but it failed to account for the fact that switched branches were already improving faster than non-switched branches before the procedure change.

### 2. Parallel Trends Assumption Violated
A key requirement for valid causal inference is that treatment and control groups follow similar trends before the intervention. This was **not** the case here:

| Group | Pre-Treatment Trend (points/week) |
|-------|-----------------------------------|
| Switched branches | +0.53 (p<0.001) |
| Non-switched branches | +0.24 (p=0.002) |

Switched branches were improving at **more than twice the rate** of non-switched branches even before the procedure change. This suggests the branches selected for the new procedure may have differed systematically (e.g., more motivated staff, better management, or other unmeasured factors).

### 3. No Effect on Staffing or Visits
The procedure did not significantly affect staffing levels or visitor volume, which is neutral news—but also means we cannot point to operational mechanisms that might explain any service improvement.

### 4. Study Design Limitations
- Branches were **not randomly assigned** to switch procedures
- Switched branches may have been selected based on characteristics that also affect service scores
- The 17-week observation period may be too short to detect longer-term effects

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Pause the rollout** until more conclusive evidence is available.

2. **Design a randomized controlled trial** for the next phase:
   - Randomly assign branches to new vs. old procedure
   - This eliminates selection bias and ensures valid causal inference

3. **Extend the observation period** to 6–12 months to capture:
   - Learning curve effects (staff may need time to master the new procedure)
   - Long-term sustainability of any effects

4. **Investigate why switched branches were improving faster** before the procedure change:
   - Were they selected based on performance metrics?
   - Do they have different characteristics (size, location, staffing)?
   - Understanding this could inform better implementation strategies

5. **Consider qualitative research** (staff interviews, site visits) to understand:
   - How the new procedure is actually being implemented
   - Whether staff perceive benefits or challenges
   - What adaptations might improve effectiveness
