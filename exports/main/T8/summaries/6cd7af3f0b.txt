# **Library Branch Procedure Change: Impact on Service Scores**

**Prepared for the Board of Directors**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure produced a statistically significant improvement in service scores** (+2.1 points, p = 0.0002), contradicting our initial expectation of no effect. The improvement is consistent across all post-switch weeks but modest in magnitude (approximately 4%). **Before approving a system-wide rollout, the Board should weigh this benefit against implementation costs.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### **1. Measurable Improvement in Service Scores**
- **Treatment effect:** +2.13 points on the service score index
- **95% confidence interval:** 1.01 to 3.26 points
- **Statistical significance:** p = 0.0002 (highly significant)
- **Relative improvement:** Approximately 4% increase from the baseline average of 52.4

### **2. Study Design & Data Quality**
- **150 branches** tracked over **17 weeks** (weeks 6–22)
- **90 branches** adopted the new procedure; **60 branches** maintained the old procedure
- **Complete data:** All branches reported for all 17 weeks
- **Switch occurred:** Week 15, allowing 9 weeks of pre-switch and 8 weeks of post-switch observation

### **3. Validity of the Analysis**
- **Parallel trends confirmed:** Before the switch, both groups showed similar trends in service scores. This validates our comparison method.
- **No confounding changes:** Staffing levels and visitor volumes did not change differently between the two groups, suggesting the improvement is attributable to the procedure change itself.

### **4. What the Data Shows**

| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Control (old procedure) | 52.05 | 51.18 | −0.86 |
| Treatment (new procedure) | 52.58 | 53.86 | +1.28 |
| **Difference-in-Differences** | | | **+2.14** |

The control group experienced a slight decline in service scores over time, while the treatment group improved—making the net effect of the new procedure even more pronounced.

### **5. Consistency of Effect**

The treatment group outperformed the control group in **every single week** after the switch:

| Week | Treatment − Control |
|------|---------------------|
| 15 | +2.26 |
| 16 | +2.84 |
| 17 | +2.47 |
| 18 | +2.08 |
| 19 | +3.55 |
| 20 | +2.47 |
| 21 | +3.18 |
| 22 | +2.58 |

This consistency strengthens confidence that the effect is real and not due to random variation.

## **Final Verdict**

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **Did the new procedure change service scores?** | **Yes** — the effect is statistically significant and consistent. |
| **Was our initial expectation correct?** | **No** — we expected no difference, but the data shows a clear positive effect. |
| **Is the effect large enough to justify rollout?** | **Uncertain** — a 4% improvement is meaningful but modest. The Board should consider: |
| | • What is the cost of implementing the new procedure at all branches? |
| | • What is the cost of *not* improving service scores? |
| | • Are there operational trade-offs (staff time, training, complexity)? |
| **Recommendation** | **Proceed with rollout IF** implementation costs are low to moderate. If costs are substantial, consider a phased rollout or further cost-benefit analysis. |

## **Conclusion**

The evidence is clear: **the new procedure improves service scores.** Our initial assumption—that this change would make no difference—was incorrect. The effect is real, consistent, and statistically robust. 

However, statistical significance does not automatically justify a system-wide rollout. The Board must now decide whether a 2-point (4%) improvement represents sufficient value relative to implementation costs.
