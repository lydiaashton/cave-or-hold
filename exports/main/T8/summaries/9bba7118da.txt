# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

---

## **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

**Date:** Analysis of weeks 6–22 service score data  
**Prepared for:** Board Decision on System-Wide Rollout

---

### **BOTTOM LINE**

**The evidence does not support a confident conclusion that the new procedure improves service scores.** While branches that switched show higher post-period scores, this appears to reflect **pre-existing differences** rather than a causal effect of the procedure. Branches that chose to switch were already improving faster than control branches *before* the switch occurred.

**Recommendation:** Do **NOT** proceed with system-wide rollout based on this evidence. Instead, conduct a properly randomized trial.

---

### **FINAL VERDICT**

> **❌ DO NOT APPROVE SYSTEM-WIDE ROLLOUT**
>
> **Reason:** The data does not demonstrate that the new procedure *causes* improved service scores. The observed association is confounded by pre-existing differences between branches that chose to switch and those that did not.
>
> **What would change this verdict:**
> - A randomized controlled trial showing positive effects, OR
> - Additional data showing a clear discontinuity at the switch point that cannot be explained by pre-trends
>
> **Risk of proceeding anyway:** The cooperative may invest resources in a procedure that provides no actual benefit, while diverting attention from interventions that could genuinely improve service.

---

### **KEY FINDINGS**

| Finding | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Observed post-switch difference** | Treatment branches scored +1.8 points higher (regression estimate) |
| **BUT: Pre-existing trend** | Treatment branches were already gaining +0.32 points/week *before* switching |
| **At the switch point (Week 14→15)** | Only +0.07 points change—no observable jump |
| **Parallel trends assumption** | **Violated**—critical threat to causal interpretation |
| **Likely explanation** | Selection bias: branches that switched were already on improvement trajectory |

**What the data actually shows:**

```
Week 14 (last pre-week):  Treatment - Control = +1.79 points
Week 15 (first post-week): Treatment - Control = +1.87 points
Change at switch:          Only +0.07 points
```

If the new procedure caused improvement, we would expect to see a noticeable jump at Week 15. **We do not see this.**

---

### **WHY THE INITIAL ANALYSIS WAS MISLEADING**

The difference-in-differences method requires that treatment and control groups would have followed **parallel trends** absent the intervention. This assumption is **not met**:

| Period | Treatment vs. Control Gap |
|--------|---------------------------|
| Week 6 | -1.08 points (treatment *behind*) |
| Week 14 | +1.79 points (treatment *ahead*) |
| **Pre-period change** | **+2.87 points over 9 weeks** |

The treatment group was closing the gap and surpassing control branches *before* any procedure change. The regression attributes this entire divergent trend to the "treatment effect," but much of it predates the switch.

---

### **RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS**

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
| **1. Do NOT roll out system-wide** | Current evidence does not establish causal benefit |
| **2. Run a randomized controlled trial** | Randomly assign branches to new vs. old procedure to eliminate selection bias |
| **3. If rollout proceeds anyway, implement rigorous monitoring** | Track whether service scores actually improve post-conversion; be prepared to reverse course |
| **4. Investigate why switching branches were already improving** | May reveal other factors (management quality, local initiatives) that could be leveraged |
| **5. Re-convene board after 12 weeks of additional data** | Allow time for proper experimental design |

---

### **ANALYTICAL APPROACH & LIMITATIONS**

This analysis uses difference-in-differences regression with cluster-robust standard errors. However, the **parallel trends assumption is violated**, meaning the estimated +1.8 point "effect" cannot be interpreted as causal. The observed association likely reflects **selection bias**—branches that opted into the new procedure were systematically different from those that did not.

A randomized assignment of branches to procedures would resolve this limitation.
