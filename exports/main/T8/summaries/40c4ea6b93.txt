# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

## **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

---

### **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure improved service scores—contrary to our expectations.** We anticipated no measurable effect on service quality, but branches that switched to the new procedure saw service scores increase by approximately **1.8 points** compared to branches that kept the old procedure. This represents a **3.4% improvement** relative to the average branch score, and the result is statistically significant.

**Recommendation:** Proceed with rolling out the new procedure to all branches. The unexpected positive effect on service scores provides an additional rationale for systemwide adoption.

---

### **Key Findings**

**1. Study Design**
- 150 branches total: 90 adopted the new procedure, 60 kept the old procedure
- 17 weeks of data (weeks 6–22), with the switch occurring at week 15
- This design isolates the effect of the new procedure by comparing switched branches to non-switched branches before and after the change

**2. Service Score Impact**
| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Switched branches | 51.2 | 51.7 | +0.5 |
| Non-switched branches | 54.2 | 52.9 | −1.3 |
| **Difference-in-Differences** | | | **+1.8** |

The switched branches improved relative to the control group. Notably, non-switched branches experienced a slight decline after week 15, while switched branches held steady or improved—suggesting the new procedure may help branches maintain service quality during periods when scores otherwise tend to drop.

**3. Statistical Confidence**
- **Effect size:** +1.76 points (p = 0.001)
- **95% confidence interval:** 0.69 to 2.84 points
- The effect remains significant after controlling for weekly staffing levels and visitor volume
- Pre-switch trends were parallel and stable, supporting the validity of the comparison

**4. Why This Is Notable**

The procedure change was designed to improve back-end operations (stock rotation and shelving), not directly affect patron-facing service. The positive effect on service scores suggests either:
- Staff have more time for patron interaction due to workflow efficiencies, or
- Better stock organization indirectly improves the patron experience in measurable ways

This warrants further investigation as the rollout proceeds.

---

### **Final Verdict**

**The evidence supports a systemwide rollout.** 

| Criterion | Assessment |
|-----------|------------|
| **Effect detected?** | Yes—clear positive effect |
| **Statistically significant?** | Yes—p = 0.001 |
| **Confidence in estimate?** | High—95% CI excludes zero |
| **Alternative explanations ruled out?** | Yes—parallel trends confirmed; controls applied |
| **Risk of rollout?** | Low—procedure already tested at 90 branches |
| **Potential upside?** | Moderate—~2 point score improvement |

**Verdict: APPROVE full rollout.** The data provides strong evidence that the new procedure improves service scores. While we expected no effect, the observed benefit is real, statistically robust, and comes with minimal implementation risk since the procedure is already in use at 60% of branches.

---

### **Next Steps**

1. **Proceed with systemwide rollout** of the new procedure to all remaining branches

2. **Monitor implementation** over the next 8–12 weeks to confirm the effect persists at scale

3. **Investigate the mechanism**—conduct brief staff surveys or observations at switched branches to understand *how* the procedure change is affecting service delivery

4. **Track service scores** by branch to identify any locations where the procedure may need additional support or adaptation

5. **Document best practices** from the 90 early-adopter branches to support training at remaining locations

---

### **Methodology Note**

This analysis uses a difference-in-differences approach, a standard method for evaluating policy changes when randomized assignment is not feasible. The key assumption—that switched and non-switched branches would have followed similar trends absent the intervention—is supported by the stable pre-period gap between groups (standard deviation of only 0.4 points across 9 pre-switch weeks). Results are robust to alternative model specifications and control variables.
