## **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

---

### **BOTTOM LINE**

**The evidence does not support a confident conclusion that the new procedure caused the improvement.** While branches that adopted the new procedure showed higher service scores after the switch, these same branches were already improving relative to others *before* the procedure changed. This pattern makes it impossible to separate the effect of the new procedure from pre-existing differences between the groups.

**Recommendation: Do not proceed with a full rollout at this time.** Additional evidence is needed before committing system-wide resources.

---

### **KEY FINDINGS**

#### **1. What the Data Shows**

| Period | Control Branches | Treatment Branches | Gap |
|--------|------------------|-------------------|-----|
| Week 6 (earliest) | Baseline | Baseline | 0.0 |
| Week 10 | — | — | +1.1 |
| Week 14 (before switch) | — | — | +2.2 |
| Week 22 (latest) | — | — | +3.8 |

**Critical observation:** The gap between treatment and control branches was already **2.2 points** by week 14—the week *before* the new procedure began.

#### **2. Why This Matters**

The standard method for evaluating policy changes (difference-in-differences) requires that treatment and control groups would have followed similar trends if not for the intervention. **This assumption is not met.** Treatment branches were on an upward trajectory before the switch.

This could mean:
- Branches selected for the new procedure were already improving for other reasons
- Higher-performing branches were chosen to pilot the new procedure
- Some unmeasured factor affected treatment branches differently

#### **3. The Observed Improvement Cannot Be Attributed to the Procedure**

The main analysis estimated a 2.03-point treatment effect. However, given the pre-existing divergence:
- **This estimate likely overstates the true effect**
- **We cannot rule out that the effect is zero**
- **The procedure may still help, but this data cannot prove it**

#### **4. What We Can Say**

- Treatment branches ended the period with higher service scores than control branches
- The gap widened over the observation period
- The new procedure *may* contribute to this, but we lack credible evidence

---

### **FINAL VERDICT**

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Did service scores improve at treatment branches? | **Yes** |
| Can we attribute this to the new procedure? | **No** |
| Is the evidence strong enough for system-wide rollout? | **No** |
| Should we abandon the new procedure? | **Not necessarily** |

**Bottom line:** The new procedure remains promising but unproven. A full rollout would be premature.

---

### **RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS**

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
| **Pause system-wide rollout** | Avoid committing resources to an unproven intervention |
| **Run a randomized pilot** | Randomly assign additional branches to treatment/control to establish causal evidence |
| **Extend monitoring period** | Track current treatment branches for 3-6 more months to see if gains persist |
| **Investigate selection criteria** | Understand why treatment branches were improving before the switch |
| **Reconvene in 90 days** | Review new evidence before making rollout decision |

---

### **ABOUT THIS ANALYSIS**

This evaluation used a **difference-in-differences** design with 12,053 branch-week observations across 709 branches. An event study analysis revealed that the key identifying assumption (parallel pre-trends) was violated, undermining causal claims.

**Analytical limitation:** Without random assignment or parallel pre-trends, observational data cannot reliably isolate the effect of the new procedure from other factors.
