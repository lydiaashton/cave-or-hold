# **MEMORANDUM**

**To:** Library Cooperative Board  
**From:** Data Analysis Team  
**Date:** November 2026  
**Subject:** Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure

---

## **Bottom Line**

**We cannot confidently conclude the new procedure caused the improvement.** While branches that switched show higher service scores after the change, the analysis reveals that treatment and control branches were already on **different trajectories before the switch**. This violates a key assumption of our evaluation method, meaning the estimated 2-point improvement may reflect pre-existing trends rather than the procedure itself. **We recommend against a full rollout until more reliable evidence is gathered.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### What the Data Shows
| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Raw DiD estimate | +1.98 service score points |
| Statistical significance | p < 0.001 |
| Post-switch treatment average | 53.75 |
| Post-switch control average | 51.23 |

### The Critical Problem: Parallel Trends Violated

The difference-in-differences method requires that treatment and control groups would have followed similar trends without the intervention. **This assumption is not met:**

| Group | Pre-Switch Weekly Trend | P-value |
|-------|------------------------|---------|
| Control (old procedure) | -0.12 points/week | 0.018 |
| Treatment (new procedure) | +0.16 points/week | 0.0002 |

**The treatment group was already improving relative to control before the switch.** The gap between groups widened by 1.84 points during the pre-switch period alone—nearly as large as the estimated "treatment effect."

### Pre-Switch Gap Evolution

| Week | Treatment − Control |
|------|---------------------|
| 6 | -0.18 |
| 10 | +0.31 |
| 14 | +1.66 |
| 22 | +3.41 |

The gap was widening before week 15 and continued widening after. We cannot isolate how much (if any) is due to the procedure.

---

## **What This Means**

1. **The 2-point estimate is likely biased upward.** The true effect could be smaller, zero, or even negative.

2. **Branches self-selected into treatment.** The 425 branches that switched may differ systematically from the 284 that didn't (e.g., more motivated staff, better management, different patron demographics).

3. **Statistical significance ≠ causal proof.** The p-value indicates the association is unlikely to be random, but it doesn't prove the procedure caused the improvement.

---

## **Recommendations**

### Do NOT proceed with full rollout yet. Instead:

1. **Pause system-wide implementation** until causal evidence is stronger.

2. **Consider a randomized rollout** for remaining branches. Randomly assign half to adopt the procedure and half to wait. This would restore the ability to estimate causal effects.

3. **Collect more data.** Extend the observation period to see if the gap stabilizes, widens further, or reverses.

4. **Investigate selection factors.** Understand why certain branches chose to switch. Are they systematically different in ways that affect service scores?

5. **Gather qualitative feedback.** Interview staff at switched branches about implementation challenges and perceived benefits.

6. **Re-evaluate in 3–6 months** with additional data and/or a randomized design.

---

## **Final Verdict**

> **DO NOT ROLL OUT SYSTEM-WIDE AT THIS TIME.**
>
> The evidence is insufficient to support a causal claim that the new procedure improves service scores. The branches that adopted the procedure were already outperforming control branches before the switch, and this pre-existing advantage explains at least some—possibly all—of the observed post-switch difference.
>
> **Proceeding with a full rollout would be premature.** The cooperative risks investing significant resources in a change that may not deliver meaningful benefits. A randomized evaluation or extended observation period is needed before making a system-wide commitment.
