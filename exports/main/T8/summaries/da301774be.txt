# **Library Branch Procedure Change: Impact Analysis**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure is associated with higher service scores, but confidence in the exact magnitude is limited.** Branches that adopted the new procedure maintained service scores approximately **2 points higher** than control branches after the switch. However, treatment branches were already improving relative to control branches *before* the switch, which makes it difficult to isolate how much of the post-switch difference is due to the procedure itself versus pre-existing trends. We recommend a **cautious, phased rollout** with close monitoring.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **Study Overview**
- **709 branches** tracked over **17 weeks** (weeks 6–22)
- **425 branches** adopted the new procedure starting week 15
- **284 branches** continued with the old procedure (control group)

### **Main Results**

| Group | Before Switch (weeks 6–14) | After Switch (weeks 15–22) | Change |
|-------|---------------------------|---------------------------|--------|
| Control (old procedure) | 52.0 | 48.9 | −3.1 |
| Treatment (new procedure) | 53.0 | 51.9 | −1.1 |
| **Observed Difference** | | | **+2.0** |

### **Important Caveat: Pre-Existing Trends**

Treatment branches were already pulling ahead of control branches *before* the procedure change:

| Week | Gap (Treatment − Control) |
|------|--------------------------|
| 6 | −0.6 |
| 10 | +1.7 |
| 14 | +2.4 |

This **pre-treatment trend** (gaining ~0.3 points/week) means the standard analysis may **overstate the procedure's effect**. If this trend had continued without intervention, we would have expected some gap widening anyway.

### **Post-Switch Pattern**

After the switch, the gap stabilized rather than continuing to widen:

| Week | Gap (Treatment − Control) |
|------|--------------------------|
| 15 | +2.2 |
| 17 | +3.2 |
| 22 | +3.4 |

This suggests the new procedure may have **helped treatment branches maintain their advantage** during a period when all branches saw declining scores, rather than creating a new advantage.

### **Statistical Summary**

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Standard DiD estimate | +2.0 points |
| 95% confidence interval | +1.5 to +2.5 |
| Statistical significance | p < 0.001 |
| **Adjusted interpretation** | **Likely +0.5 to +1.5 points** (accounting for pre-trends) |

---

## **Final Verdict**

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **Did the new procedure harm service scores?** | **No** — treatment branches performed better than control branches after the switch |
| **Did the new procedure improve service scores?** | **Probably, but the effect is smaller than initial analysis suggests** |
| **Is the evidence strong enough for full rollout?** | **No** — pre-existing trends create uncertainty about the true causal effect |
| **Recommendation** | **Phased rollout with randomized assignment** to generate cleaner evidence |

**Rationale:** The new procedure shows no negative effects and likely provides modest benefits. However, because branches that volunteered to switch early were already on an upward trajectory, we cannot confidently attribute the full +2.0 point difference to the procedure itself. A randomized phased rollout will allow the cooperative to measure the true effect while still extending the new procedure to more branches.

---

## **Next Steps**

1. **Approve phased rollout** to 50–100 additional branches, with **random selection** among willing branches

2. **Extend evaluation period** to 12+ weeks post-switch per branch to distinguish procedure effects from pre-existing trends

3. **Monitor closely during transition**—the first 3–4 weeks show variable effects as staff adjust

4. **Reconvene in 12 weeks** to review results from the phased rollout before deciding on system-wide implementation

5. **Investigate the system-wide decline** in scores after week 15, which affected all branches regardless of procedure

---

## **Methodology Note**

This analysis uses difference-in-differences, which relies on the assumption that treatment and control branches would have followed parallel trends absent the intervention. The pre-treatment data shows this assumption is **not fully met**—treatment branches were already improving relative to control branches. This means the +2.0 point estimate should be interpreted as an **upper bound** on the procedure's effect.
