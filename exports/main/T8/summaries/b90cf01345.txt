# **REVISED MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Board of Directors, Library Cooperative  
**FROM:** Data Analysis Team  
**DATE:** November 15, 2026  
**SUBJECT:** New Shelving Procedure – Service Score Impact Analysis

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The evidence is inconclusive.** While branches that switched procedures showed higher service scores afterward, those same branches were already improving faster *before* the switch. This undermines our ability to claim the procedure change caused the improvement. The 2.2-point estimate likely overstates the true effect.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **What the Raw Numbers Show**

| Group | Before | After | Change |
|-------|--------|-------|--------|
| Branches keeping old procedure (284) | 51.03 | 49.87 | −1.16 |
| Branches adopting new procedure (425) | 51.00 | 52.02 | +1.02 |
| **Raw difference-in-differences** | | | **+2.19** |

### **The Critical Problem: Pre-Existing Trends**

Branches selected for the new procedure were **already improving faster** before the switch:

- Treatment branches gained **0.26 points per week more** than control branches during Weeks 6–14
- This pre-trend difference is **statistically significant (p = 0.0001)**
- Over 9 weeks, this accounts for approximately **2.3 points** of the observed effect

**This means the 2.2-point "treatment effect" may be entirely explained by pre-existing differences, not the procedure change itself.**

### **Why This Matters**

The standard analysis method (difference-in-differences) requires that treatment and control groups follow similar trends before the intervention. This assumption is **violated** in our data. Branches were not randomly assigned to switch—some systematic factor (management quality, staff engagement, local conditions) likely influenced both the selection to switch AND the service score trajectory.

---

## **Final Verdict**

### **Cannot Recommend Full Rollout Based on Current Evidence**

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Did service scores improve in switching branches? | **Yes** – clearly |
| Can we attribute this to the new procedure? | **No** – not confidently |
| Is the 2.2-point effect reliable for planning? | **No** – likely inflated |
| Should we roll out to all remaining branches now? | **No** – insufficient evidence |

### **Reasoning**

1. **Selection bias is the most plausible explanation.** Branches chosen to switch (or that volunteered) appear to have been on a stronger trajectory regardless of the procedure change.

2. **The pre-trend alone explains the entire observed effect.** Approximately 2.3 points of pre-existing momentum vs. 2.2 points of observed "treatment effect."

3. **Acting on inflated expectations creates risk.** If the true effect is zero or modest, a full rollout would incur training costs and operational disruption without delivering anticipated benefits.

4. **Better evidence is achievable.** A randomized or phased rollout of remaining branches would create the clean comparison needed to isolate the true effect.

---

## **Recommendation**

**Pause the full rollout decision and pursue one of these paths:**

### **Option A: Rigorous Test (Recommended)**
Randomly assign the remaining 284 branches to switch now vs. in 8–12 weeks. This creates a clean experimental design that will yield a credible causal estimate.

### **Option B: Cautious Rollout with Monitoring**
Proceed with rollout but:
- Set expectations at 0–1.0 points (not 2.2)
- Monitor weekly and establish clear success thresholds
- Be prepared to pause or adjust if results don't materialize

### **Option C: Qualitative Investigation**
Interview early-adopter branches to understand what else may have contributed to their improvement (training, leadership, local factors). This may reveal that the procedure works best under certain conditions.
