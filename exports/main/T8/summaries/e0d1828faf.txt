# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

## **Evaluation of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

---

### **Bottom Line**

**We cannot conclude the new procedure improved service scores.** Branches that adopted the new procedure were already improving faster than other branches before the switch occurred. Once we account for this pre-existing trend, there is **no detectable additional benefit** from the procedure change. We recommend **against system-wide rollout** until stronger evidence is obtained through a properly designed evaluation.

---

### **Key Findings**

**1. Pre-Existing Trends Violate Analysis Assumptions**
| Period | Control Trend | Treatment Trend | Difference |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|------------|
| Before Switch (Weeks 6-14) | +0.52 pts/week | +0.77 pts/week | **+0.25 pts/week** |

- Treatment branches were improving **significantly faster** before the switch (p < 0.001)
- This violates the core assumption needed to attribute post-switch differences to the procedure

**2. Expected vs. Observed Gap Changes**
| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Pre-period average gap (T-C) | 0.51 points |
| Post-period average gap (T-C) | 2.34 points |
| Observed gap increase | 1.84 points |
| **Expected increase from pre-trends alone** | **2.01 points** |
| **Residual (potential treatment effect)** | **-0.17 points** |

**3. What This Means**
- The entire observed improvement is explained by pre-existing trends
- There is **no statistical evidence** the new procedure caused additional improvement
- The procedure may have had zero effect, or even a slight negative effect

**4. Why This Happened**
- Branches were not randomly assigned to switch procedures
- Branches that switched may have been:
  - Better managed overall
  - More motivated to improve
  - Selected because they were already on an upward trajectory
- This creates selection bias that invalidates simple before/after comparisons

---

### **Final Verdict**

> **❌ DO NOT PROCEED WITH SYSTEM-WIDE ROLLOUT**
>
> **Reason:** The analysis fails to demonstrate that the new procedure *causes* improved service scores. The observed improvements are fully explained by pre-existing differences between branches that chose to switch versus those that did not.
>
> **What would change this verdict:** A randomized controlled trial showing statistically significant improvement (p < 0.05) with a minimum effect size of 1.0 point on the service score index.

---

### **Next Steps**

| Priority | Action | Timeline |
|----------|--------|----------|
| **1** | **Pause all expansion** of the new procedure | Immediate |
| **2** | Design a randomized pilot (50% treatment, 50% control) | 30 days |
| **3** | Run pilot with pre-registered success metrics | 90 days |
| **4** | Return to Board with causal evidence | 120 days |
| **5** | Make final rollout decision based on pilot results | 150 days |

---

### **Recommendation**

**Vote: AGAINST system-wide rollout at this time.**

The cooperative should not commit resources to expanding a procedure whose effectiveness has not been demonstrated. Instead, invest in a rigorous evaluation that will provide actionable evidence. If the procedure truly works, a randomized pilot will confirm it. If it does not, this approach prevents wasteful spending on an ineffective change.

---

### **Conclusion**

The data does not support the conclusion that the new procedure improves service scores. The observed improvements reflect pre-existing differences between branches that did and did not adopt the procedure, not the effect of the procedure itself. Rolling out system-wide based on this evidence would risk investing resources in a change that may provide no benefit. A properly designed evaluation is needed before making expansion decisions.
