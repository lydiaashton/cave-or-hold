# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

**Subject:** Evaluation of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure  
**Date:** November 2026  
**Prepared by:** Data Analysis Office

---

## **BOTTOM LINE**

**We recommend against rolling out the new procedure to all branches at this time.**

The data does not provide credible evidence that the new procedure improves service scores. Branches that adopted the new procedure were already improving *before* they switched. After accounting for this pre-existing trend, the new procedure shows **no measurable benefit** and may be associated with slightly worse performance than expected.

---

## **KEY FINDINGS**

### 1. What the Surface Data Shows

At first glance, the new procedure appears successful:

| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Switched branches | 50.4 | 52.8 | +2.4 |
| Non-switched branches | 48.7 | 48.8 | +0.1 |
| **Apparent effect** | | | **+2.3 points** |

### 2. The Critical Problem

Branches that chose to switch were **already improving faster** than other branches before the procedure changed:

- **Week 6:** Switching branches scored only 0.4 points higher than non-switching branches
- **Week 14 (just before switch):** The gap had grown to 3.6 points
- **The gap was widening by 0.38 points per week** during this pre-treatment period

This pattern indicates **selection bias**: branches that opted into the new procedure were already on an upward trajectory, likely due to better management, more motivated staff, or other advantages unrelated to the procedure itself.

### 3. What the Evidence Actually Shows

When we account for the pre-existing trend, the picture changes:

| Measure | Finding |
|---------|---------|
| If pre-trend had continued (no procedure change) | Expected gap: 4.95 points |
| Actual post-switch gap | 3.96 points |
| **Estimated procedure effect** | **-1.0 points** |

The switching branches actually performed **worse than their own pre-existing trend would have predicted** after adopting the new procedure.

### 4. Statistical Confidence

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Is the simple difference statistically significant? | Yes (p<0.001) |
| Does this represent a causal effect? | **No**—parallel trends assumption violated |
| Can we trust the +2.3 point estimate? | **No**—it reflects pre-existing differences, not the procedure |
| Is there credible evidence of benefit? | **No** |

---

## **RECOMMENDATIONS**

### Immediate Actions

1. **Pause the systemwide rollout.** Proceeding based on current evidence risks implementing a change that provides no benefit while disrupting operations.

2. **Investigate the switching branches.** Since they were already improving, identify what factors drove that improvement (management practices, staffing, community engagement, etc.). These insights may be more valuable than the procedure itself.

3. **Collect qualitative feedback.** Survey branch staff about their experience with the new procedure—implementation challenges, time requirements, and perceived impact on service quality.

### For Stronger Evidence

4. **Conduct a randomized trial.** Randomly assign remaining branches to adopt the procedure now vs. later. This eliminates selection bias and provides credible causal evidence.

5. **Extend the evaluation period.** Continue tracking all branches for at least 6 more months to assess whether effects stabilize, grow, or diminish over time.

6. **Examine heterogeneous effects.** The procedure may work better for certain branch types (by size, location, or staffing levels). Targeted rollout may be more appropriate than systemwide adoption.

---

## **DECISION FRAMEWORK**

| If Randomized Trial Shows... | Then... |
|------------------------------|---------|
| Positive, significant effect | Proceed with systemwide rollout |
| No significant effect | Abandon the new procedure; focus on other improvements |
| Negative effect | Revert switching branches to the old procedure |

---

## **CONCLUSION**

The current evidence reflects **which branches chose to switch**, not **what the procedure accomplishes**. Rolling out the procedure systemwide based on this data would be premature and potentially costly.

We recommend pausing the rollout, conducting a proper randomized evaluation, and learning from the branches that were already improving—regardless of the procedure they use.
