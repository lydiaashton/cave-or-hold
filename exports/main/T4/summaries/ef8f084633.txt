# MEMORANDUM

**TO:** Board of Directors, Library Cooperative  
**FROM:** Data Analysis Team  
**DATE:** November 15, 2026  
**SUBJECT:** Evaluation of New Branch Shelving and Rotation Procedure

---

## Executive Summary

After analyzing 17 weeks of service score data from all 709 branches, we find **no reliable evidence that the new shelving and rotation procedure improved service quality.** While branches that adopted the new procedure showed higher scores after the switch, this pattern existed before the change was implemented. We recommend **against** a system-wide rollout at this time.

---

## Bottom Line

**Do not proceed with system-wide rollout.** The apparent improvement in service scores at treated branches reflects pre-existing differences between branches, not a causal effect of the new procedure. More rigorous statistical analyses that account for these differences show no significant benefit from the procedure change.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. Surface-Level Results Are Misleading

| Period | Control Branches (284) | Treated Branches (425) |
|--------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Before switch (weeks 6-14) | 50.43 | 51.80 |
| After switch (weeks 15-22) | 52.46 | 55.55 |
| **Change** | **+2.02** | **+3.75** |

The simple comparison suggests a 1.7-point benefit from the new procedure. However, this comparison is invalid because treated and control branches were already on different trajectories.

### 2. Pre-Existing Trends Undermine the Analysis

**Critical finding:** Before the procedure change, treated branches were already improving faster than control branches—approximately 0.27 points per week more. Over the 8-week pre-period, this created a widening gap that had nothing to do with the new procedure.

This violates the fundamental assumption required for causal inference: that treated and control branches would have followed similar trends absent the intervention.

### 3. Rigorous Analyses Show No Clear Effect

When we apply methods that better account for pre-existing differences:

| Method | Estimated Effect | Conclusion |
|--------|-----------------|------------|
| Simple comparison | +1.73 points | Appears positive |
| Two-way fixed effects | +1.71 points | Appears positive |
| Narrow window around switch | +0.88 points | Weak evidence |
| **Segmented regression** | **-0.37 points** | **No effect** |
| **First-difference model** | **-0.40 points** | **No effect** |

The more credible methods show no statistically significant improvement attributable to the procedure change.

### 4. Visual Evidence Supports This Conclusion

The week-by-week data shows:
- No noticeable "jump" in treated branch scores at week 15 (when the switch occurred)
- The gap between treated and control branches continued widening after the switch at roughly the same rate as before
- Week-over-week changes were similar for both groups throughout the study period

---

## Why This Happened

The branches selected for the initial rollout appear to have been systematically different from those that were not. Possible explanations include:

- **Selection bias:** Higher-performing or better-resourced branches may have been chosen first
- **Motivation effects:** Branches eager to improve may have volunteered for the new procedure
- **Unobserved factors:** Other characteristics (staffing, location, patron demographics) may drive both selection and performance trends

Without random assignment, we cannot separate these factors from the procedure's actual effect.

---

## Recommendations

### Immediate Actions

1. **Halt system-wide rollout** pending further evidence
2. **Document the selection process** used for the initial rollout to understand potential biases
3. **Continue monitoring** both groups with ongoing data collection

### For Future Procedure Changes

4. **Use randomized assignment** when testing new procedures. Randomly assign branches to treatment and control groups to ensure valid comparisons.

5. **Establish baseline equivalence** before implementation. Verify that treatment and control groups have similar pre-intervention trends.

6. **Plan for longer evaluation periods.** Eight weeks of post-implementation data may be insufficient to detect lasting effects.

7. **Consider subgroup analysis.** The procedure may benefit certain branch types more than others. Future analysis should examine effects by branch size, location, and baseline performance.

### If Board Wishes to Proceed Anyway

Should the board decide to move forward despite the inconclusive evidence:

- Implement a **phased rollout** with clear milestones
- Define **success metrics** and thresholds for continuation
- Build in **evaluation checkpoints** with authority to pause or reverse
- Commit to **rigorous ongoing monitoring**

---

## Methodology Summary

- **Data:** 12,053 weekly observations from 709 branches (weeks 6-22, 2026)
- **Treatment:** 425 branches adopted new procedure starting week 15
- **Control:** 284 branches continued with existing procedure
- **Analysis:** Difference-in-differences, fixed effects models, event study, segmented regression
- **Statistical standards:** Cluster-robust standard errors (clustered by branch)
