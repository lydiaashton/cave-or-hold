# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**

## **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

### **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure produced an unexpected benefit.** Although the procedure change was not expected to affect service scores, branches that switched saw scores increase by approximately **1.8 points** (95% confidence interval: 0.7 to 2.8 points) compared to branches that kept the old procedure. This represents a **3.4% improvement** relative to the average branch score. The result is statistically significant and robust to controlling for staffing levels and visitor volume.

**Recommendation:** Proceed with rolling out the new procedure to all branches. This is a low-cost improvement that was not anticipated but delivers measurable value.

---

### **Key Findings**

**1. Study Design**
- 150 branches total: 90 adopted the new procedure, 60 kept the old procedure
- 17 weeks of data (weeks 6–22), with the switch occurring at week 15
- This "natural experiment" design allows us to isolate the effect of the new procedure by comparing switched branches to non-switched branches before and after the change

**2. Service Score Impact**
| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Switched branches | 51.2 | 51.7 | +0.5 |
| Non-switched branches | 54.2 | 52.9 | −1.3 |
| **Difference-in-Differences** | | | **+1.8** |

The switched branches improved relative to the control group. Notably, the control group's scores declined slightly after week 15, while switched branches held steady or improved—suggesting the new procedure may have helped branches maintain service quality during a period when scores otherwise tended to drop.

**3. Statistical Confidence**
- **Effect size:** +1.76 points (p = 0.001)
- **95% confidence interval:** 0.69 to 2.84 points
- The effect remains significant after controlling for weekly staffing levels and visitor volume
- The parallel trends assumption is satisfied: before the switch, the gap between switched and non-switched branches was stable (standard deviation of only 0.4 points across 9 pre-switch weeks)

**4. Why This Matters**

This finding is notable because the procedure change was designed for operational efficiency, not service quality. The positive effect on service scores appears to be an unanticipated co-benefit. Possible explanations worth exploring:

- Staff may have more time for patron assistance under the new shelving system
- Improved stock organization may make it easier to locate materials quickly
- The rotation schedule may reduce gaps in available inventory

---

### **Next Steps**

1. **Proceed with systemwide rollout** — This is a no-regrets decision: the procedure was already planned for adoption, and we now have evidence of an additional benefit

2. **Investigate the mechanism** — Conduct brief interviews or surveys with staff at switched branches to understand *why* service scores improved. This could reveal best practices to emphasize during training

3. **Monitor implementation** over the next 8–12 weeks to confirm the effect persists at scale

4. **Track service scores by branch** to identify any locations where the procedure may need additional support

5. **Update expectations** — Document this finding for future operational changes; procedure changes may have service quality implications even when not designed for that purpose

---

### **Methodology Note**

This analysis uses a difference-in-differences approach, a standard method for evaluating policy changes when randomized assignment is not feasible. The key assumption—that switched and non-switched branches would have followed similar trends absent the intervention—is supported by the stable pre-period gap between groups. Results are robust to alternative model specifications and control variables.
