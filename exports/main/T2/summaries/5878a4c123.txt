# **Library Branch Procedure Change: Impact on Service Scores**

## **Bottom Line**

**The new shelving and rotation procedure did not demonstrably improve service scores.** While branches using the new procedure showed higher score improvements than control branches, this difference was already occurring *before* the switch. After accounting for pre-existing trends, there is no statistically significant evidence that the new procedure caused any improvement. **We recommend against a system-wide rollout until further evidence is gathered.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### **Study Design**
- **709 branches** analyzed over 17 weeks (weeks 6–22)
- **425 branches** adopted the new procedure at week 15 (treatment group)
- **284 branches** continued with the old procedure (control group)
- Service scores average ~50 points; higher is better

### **What the Data Shows**

| Metric | Control Group | Treatment Group |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| Average score before switch | 48.1 | 50.5 |
| Average score after switch | 49.2 | 53.6 |
| Raw improvement | +1.1 points | +3.1 points |
| **Apparent difference** | | **+1.9 points** |

### **The Critical Problem: Pre-Existing Trends**

The treatment branches were already improving faster than control branches *before* the procedure change:

- **Pre-switch trend**: Treatment branches improved **0.35 points/week faster** than control (p=0.0003)
- The performance gap grew from **1.1 points** (week 6) to **3.7 points** (week 14) *before* the switch
- This trend continued after the switch, but there was **no discontinuous jump** at week 15

### **Adjusted Analysis**

When we account for the pre-existing trend difference:

| Statistic | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Adjusted treatment effect | **-0.6 points** |
| 95% Confidence Interval | [-1.99, +0.86] |
| P-value | **0.43** (not significant) |

**Interpretation**: The confidence interval includes zero and modest negative effects. We cannot rule out that the new procedure has no effect—or even a slight negative effect—on service scores.

---

## **Next Steps**

### **1. Do Not Proceed with System-Wide Rollout**
The current evidence does not support expanding the new procedure to all branches. The apparent benefits are attributable to pre-existing differences between branches, not the procedure itself.

### **2. Investigate Branch Selection**
Treatment branches had higher baseline scores and were already improving faster. This suggests branches were **not randomly selected** for the new procedure. Understanding the selection criteria is essential:
- Were higher-performing branches chosen to pilot the new procedure?
- If so, this creates selection bias that invalidates simple comparisons

### **3. Consider a Proper Randomized Trial**
To definitively assess the procedure's impact:
- Randomly assign branches to treatment and control
- Ensure parallel pre-trends before measuring effects
- Run for sufficient duration to detect meaningful changes

### **4. Monitor Existing Pilot Branches**
Continue tracking the 425 treatment branches to see if effects emerge over a longer horizon. However, without a proper counterfactual, conclusions will remain limited.

### **5. Examine Other Outcomes**
Service score is one metric. Consider analyzing:
- Operational efficiency (staff time, costs)
- Patron satisfaction surveys
- Circulation or visit patterns

## **Technical Note**

This analysis used a difference-in-differences approach, the standard method for evaluating policy changes with treatment and control groups. The key assumption—that treatment and control groups would have followed parallel trends absent the intervention—**was violated** in this case. The adjusted estimates account for this violation using trend controls, but the most reliable path forward is a properly randomized evaluation.
