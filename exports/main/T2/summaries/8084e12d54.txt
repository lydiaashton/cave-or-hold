# **Memorandum to the Board**

## **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

### **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure did not produce a statistically significant improvement in service scores.** While the point estimate suggests a modest positive effect (~1.6 points), the evidence is too weak to conclude the new procedure is better than the old one. We recommend **against** a full rollout at this time and suggest either continuing the pilot with more data or revising the procedure before broader implementation.

---

### **Key Findings**

**1. Study Design**
- 34 branches total: 20 adopted the new procedure (treatment), 14 kept the old procedure (control)
- 17 weeks of data (weeks 6–22); the switch occurred at week 15
- Analysis uses a difference-in-differences approach, comparing changes in treatment branches relative to control branches

**2. Service Score Results**
| Period | Control Group (Old) | Treatment Group (New) |
|--------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Pre-switch (weeks 6–14) | 54.32 | 48.92 |
| Post-switch (weeks 15–22) | 53.09 | 49.30 |
| **Change** | **-1.23** | **+0.38** |

**3. Estimated Treatment Effect**
- **Difference-in-differences estimate: +1.59 points**
- **p-value: 0.144** (not statistically significant)
- **95% Confidence Interval: [-0.54, +3.72]**

This means the true effect could reasonably be slightly negative (harmful) or moderately positive—we simply cannot tell with confidence.

**4. Event Study Analysis**
- Examined week-by-week effects after the switch
- **Zero out of 8 post-switch weeks** showed statistically significant improvements
- Effects fluctuated between -1.9 and +2.6 points with no clear pattern
- Pre-switch trends were reasonably parallel, supporting the validity of the analysis

**5. Important Context**
- Treatment branches started with lower service scores than control branches (48.9 vs. 54.3)
- This baseline difference is controlled for in the analysis, but it's worth noting
- The control group's scores declined slightly after week 15, while treatment scores remained roughly stable

---

### **Next Steps**

1. **Do not proceed with system-wide rollout** based on current evidence. The data do not support the expectation that the new procedure improves service scores.

2. **Consider extending the pilot period.** Eight weeks of post-switch data may be insufficient to detect modest effects. Additional weeks would narrow the confidence interval and provide more definitive evidence.

3. **Investigate implementation fidelity.** Survey the 20 treatment branches to confirm the new procedure was implemented as intended. Variation in execution could mask true effects.

4. **Explore heterogeneous effects.** The average effect may hide important differences. Some branch types (e.g., by size, location, or staffing) may benefit more than others.

5. **Review the procedure itself.** If the goal is to improve service scores, consider whether the procedure targets the right mechanisms. The staffing and visit indices were not strong predictors of service scores in our models.

6. **Set a decision timeline.** Recommend reconvening in 8–12 weeks with additional data, or commission a qualitative review of the procedure before making a final rollout decision.

---

### **Technical Note**

All analyses used cluster-robust standard errors (clustered by branch) to account for within-branch correlation over time. The event study design confirms no significant pre-trends, supporting the parallel trends assumption required for valid difference-in-differences estimation.
