# **Analysis of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

## **Bottom Line**

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure is associated with a **modest improvement in service scores of approximately 2 points** (about 4% relative to the baseline). This effect is **marginally statistically significant** (p ≈ 0.10) when using appropriate statistical methods that account for branch-level differences. The evidence suggests the new procedure has a positive effect, though additional data collection would strengthen confidence in this finding.

**Recommendation:** Proceed with cautious rollout while continuing to monitor results.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **Study Design**
- **34 branches** total: 20 adopted the new procedure (treatment), 14 kept the old procedure (control)
- **17 weeks of data** (weeks 6–22): 9 weeks before the switch, 8 weeks after
- Switch occurred at **week 15**

### **Service Score Results**

| Group | Pre-Switch Average | Post-Switch Average | Change |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|--------|
| Treatment (New Procedure) | 51.13 | 52.84 | **+1.71** |
| Control (Old Procedure) | 52.56 | 52.36 | **-0.20** |

**Difference-in-Differences Estimate: +1.91 points**

This means branches that switched to the new procedure improved by about 2 points more than branches that kept the old procedure, after accounting for underlying trends.

### **Statistical Confidence**
- Using rigorous panel data methods with branch fixed effects and clustered standard errors:
  - **Effect size:** +2.0 points
  - **95% Confidence Interval:** -0.4 to +4.4 points
  - **P-value:** 0.096 (marginally significant)

- The effect is positive and meaningful, but the confidence interval includes zero, indicating some uncertainty. With unadjusted standard errors, the result would be statistically significant at the 5% level (p = 0.02).

### **Supporting Factors**
- **Staffing index** is positively associated with service scores (p = 0.003), confirming the measure captures real service quality variation
- **Parallel trends assumption** appears reasonable: treatment and control groups showed similar patterns before the switch, with treatment branches consistently scoring 1–3 points lower (a pre-existing gap that the DiD method accounts for)

## **Next Steps**

2. **Continue Monitoring** — Collect at least 8–12 more weeks of data to:
   - Narrow the confidence interval around the effect estimate
   - Assess whether the improvement is sustained over time
   - Identify any branches where the procedure is not working well

3. **Investigate Heterogeneity** — Analyze whether the effect varies by:
   - Branch size or location
   - Staff experience levels
   - Patron demographics

4. **Cost-Benefit Analysis** — Quantify the operational costs of the new procedure against the service score improvement to ensure the change delivers net value.

5. **Consider Complementary Improvements** — Since staffing levels are strongly associated with service scores, consider whether staffing adjustments could amplify the benefits of the new procedure.

- Standard errors clustered at the branch level to account for within-branch correlation
- Service scores are an index where ~50 represents average performance; higher is better
