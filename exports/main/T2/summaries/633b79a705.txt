# Analysis of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure

## Bottom Line

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure is associated with a **modest improvement in service scores** (approximately +1.7 to +2.0 points on a ~50-point scale, or about 3-4%). However, this effect is **not statistically significant at conventional levels** when accounting for the clustered nature of the data. The evidence suggests a positive direction but does not provide strong confirmation that the procedure change caused the improvement.

## Key Findings

### Study Design
- **34 branches** total: 20 adopted the new procedure (treatment), 14 kept the old procedure (control)
- **17 weeks of data**: Weeks 6-14 (before switch) and Weeks 15-22 (after switch)
- Analysis uses a **difference-in-differences** approach, comparing changes in service scores between switching and non-switching branches

### Results Summary

| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Switched Branches | 47.90 | 48.53 | +0.63 |
| Non-Switched Branches | 51.95 | 50.86 | -1.09 |
| **Difference-in-Differences** | | | **+1.71** |

### Statistical Analysis
- **Point estimate**: +1.71 to +2.02 points (depending on model specification)
- **Statistical significance**: p = 0.10 to 0.14 (not significant at 5% level, marginally significant at 10% level with controls)
- **95% Confidence Interval**: Approximately -0.4 to +4.4 points
- **Effect size**: 0.18 standard deviations (small-to-moderate)

### Important Context
1. **Baseline differences**: Branches that switched started with lower service scores (47.9 vs 51.9). This is why controlling for branch-level differences is critical—simple before/after comparisons would be misleading.

2. **Parallel trends assumption**: Pre-switch trends were similar between groups (average weekly changes of -0.46 vs -0.66), supporting the validity of the analysis.

3. **Control variables**: Staffing and visit levels showed similar patterns across groups. When included in the model, visits were positively associated with service scores.

4. **What the control group tells us**: Non-switching branches actually saw service scores *decline* after week 15 (-1.09 points). The treatment branches improved slightly (+0.63). The net effect (+1.71) reflects both the treatment improvement AND the control group decline.

## Next Steps

### Recommended Actions

1. **Proceed with cautious rollout**: The positive direction of the effect, combined with the modest cost of implementation, suggests continuing the rollout is reasonable. However, set realistic expectations—the improvement is likely small.

2. **Extend the evaluation period**: Collect 8-12 more weeks of data to:
   - Increase statistical power
   - Determine if the effect persists or grows over time
   - Rule out short-term novelty effects

3. **Monitor implementation fidelity**: Ensure branches adopting the new procedure are implementing it consistently. Variation in execution could be masking larger effects.

4. **Investigate heterogeneous effects**: Some branches may benefit more than others. Analyze whether branch size, staffing levels, or baseline performance moderate the effect.

5. **Consider a phased rollout with continued evaluation**: As remaining branches adopt the procedure, continue tracking service scores to build more evidence.

### Caveats for the Board

- The analysis cannot definitively prove causation, though the research design is strong
- The confidence interval includes zero, meaning we cannot rule out no effect
- The control group's decline after week 15 may reflect seasonal factors or other changes unrelated to the procedure
- Service score is one metric; consider operational costs, staff satisfaction, and patron feedback before making a final decision
