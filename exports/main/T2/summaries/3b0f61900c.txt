# **Library Branch Procedure Change: Impact Analysis**

## **Bottom Line**

**Do not proceed with system-wide rollout of the new shelving/rotation procedure.** The data do not demonstrate that the new procedure improved service scores. Branches that switched were already outperforming and improving faster than other branches *before* the change, and there was no measurable improvement attributable to the procedure itself.

---

## **Key Findings**

### **1. Pre-Existing Differences (Critical Issue)**
- Branches selected for the new procedure were already higher-performing before the switch
- Average service score gap (switched vs. non-switched): **1.8 points** in weeks 6-13
- This gap was **widening by 0.74 points per week** even before the procedure change
- This violates the fundamental assumption needed to claim the procedure caused any improvement

### **2. No Measurable Impact at Implementation**
- At week 15 (when the switch occurred), there was **no discontinuous jump** in service scores
- The treatment-control gap actually *decreased* slightly (-0.08 points) from week 14 to 15
- Statistical test for an immediate effect: **not significant (p=0.47)**

### **3. Post-Switch Trends**
| Period | Treatment-Control Gap | Weekly Trend |
|--------|----------------------|--------------|
| Weeks 6-13 (pre) | 1.79 points avg | +0.74 pts/week |
| Weeks 15-22 (post) | 4.05 points avg | -0.23 pts/week |

- After the switch, the treatment advantage grew **more slowly** than the pre-existing trend would have predicted
- This suggests the new procedure may have actually *slowed* the improvement rate, not accelerated it

### **4. What a Simple Comparison Would Miss**
- A naive before/after comparison shows switched branches improved by ~2 points more than control branches
- However, this entirely reflects pre-existing trends, not the procedure's effect
- Proper statistical controls reveal **no causal benefit** from the new procedure

## **Technical Note**

This analysis uses a difference-in-differences framework with controls for pre-existing differential trends. The key finding is that the "parallel trends assumption" required for causal inference is violated—treatment and control branches were on different trajectories before the intervention. When properly accounting for this, the estimated effect of the procedure is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
