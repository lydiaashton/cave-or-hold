# **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

## **Bottom Line**

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure produced a **modest improvement in service scores** (+1.6 points, approximately 3%), but the effect is **not statistically significant at conventional levels** (p=0.12–0.15). The evidence suggests a positive direction of impact, but we cannot rule out that the observed improvement occurred by chance. We recommend a **cautious, phased rollout** with continued monitoring rather than an immediate system-wide implementation.

---

## **Key Findings**

### 1. Study Design
- **34 branches** tracked over **17 weeks** (weeks 6–22)
- **20 branches** adopted the new procedure starting week 15; **14 branches** continued with the old procedure (control group)
- Analysis uses a **difference-in-differences** approach, comparing changes in service scores before and after the switch between treatment and control branches

### 2. Service Score Impact
| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|---------------|--------------|--------|
| Control (no switch) | 54.3 | 53.1 | −1.2 |
| Treatment (switched) | 48.9 | 49.3 | +0.4 |
| **Net Effect (DiD)** | | | **+1.6** |

- The new procedure is associated with a **+1.6 point increase** in service scores (approximately **3% improvement** relative to the baseline average of 51)
- Statistical significance: **p = 0.12–0.15** (not significant at the standard 5% threshold; marginally significant at 10%)

### 3. Validity Checks
- **Parallel trends confirmed**: Before the switch, treatment and control branches showed similar weekly trends in service scores (p = 0.83), supporting the validity of the comparison
- **No confounding changes**: Staffing levels and visit volumes did not change differently between treatment and control branches after the switch, suggesting the observed effect is attributable to the procedure change itself

### 4. Important Context
- Treatment branches started with **lower baseline service scores** (48.9 vs. 54.3), indicating they may have been selected for the new procedure because they had more room for improvement
- The analysis controls for these baseline differences through branch fixed effects, but the non-random selection should be noted

## **Next Steps**

### Recommended Actions

1. **Proceed with phased rollout** to remaining branches, but treat this as a **continuing evaluation** rather than a final decision
   - Prioritize branches with lower current service scores, where the potential benefit appears greatest

2. **Extend the monitoring period**
   - Collect at least 8–12 additional weeks of data post-rollout
   - This will increase statistical power and clarify whether the effect is real

3. **Track implementation fidelity**
   - Document how consistently each branch applies the new procedure
   - Variation in implementation may be masking the true effect

4. **Consider complementary analysis**
   - Examine whether certain branch characteristics (size, location, staffing) moderate the effect
   - This could help target the procedure to branches most likely to benefit

5. **Set a decision checkpoint**
   - Re-evaluate after the extended monitoring period
   - If the effect remains positive but statistically uncertain, consider the cost-benefit tradeoff: a 3% service improvement may justify rollout even without definitive statistical proof, depending on implementation costs
