# **Memorandum to the Board**

## **Title: Impact of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure on Service Scores**

---

## **Bottom Line**

**Yes, the new procedure improved service quality.** Branches that adopted the new stock rotation and shelving procedure saw their service scores increase by an average of **1.9 points** compared to branches that kept the old procedure. This improvement is statistically significant and represents a meaningful gain in service quality. We recommend rolling out the new procedure to all branches.

---

## **Key Findings**

### 1. Measurable Improvement in Service Scores
- **Treatment effect:** +1.9 points on the service score index (95% confidence interval: 0.7 to 3.1 points)
- **Statistical significance:** p = 0.002 (highly significant)
- **Context:** With an average branch score of approximately 50, this represents roughly a **4% improvement** in service quality

### 2. Strong Evidence of Causality
The analysis used a rigorous difference-in-differences design comparing:
- **90 branches** that switched to the new procedure (treatment group)
- **60 branches** that kept the old procedure (control group)

Key validation checks confirm the result is reliable:
- **Parallel trends verified:** Before the switch, both groups followed similar trajectories (no pre-existing differences in trends, p = 0.42)
- **Consistent effects:** The improvement appeared across most weeks after implementation, not driven by a single outlier week
- **Specific to service quality:** No significant changes detected in staffing levels or visit volumes, indicating the effect reflects genuine service improvements rather than other operational changes

### 3. Diverging Trajectories
| Group | Before Switch | After Switch | Change |
|-------|--------------|--------------|--------|
| Control (old procedure) | 51.2 | 50.2 | **-1.0** |
| Treated (new procedure) | 50.8 | 51.5 | **+0.7** |

Notably, branches using the old procedure experienced a slight decline in service scores over the same period, while switched branches improved. This suggests the new procedure may also help **prevent service degradation** that otherwise occurs over time.

### 4. Effect Size
- The improvement (Cohen's d = 0.19) is a **small-to-moderate effect**—meaningful for an operational change that doesn't require additional staffing or budget
- Benefits appeared quickly (within the first week) and persisted through the 8-week observation period

## **Next Steps**

### Recommended Actions

1. **Proceed with system-wide rollout** of the new procedure to all remaining branches

2. **Develop implementation support:**
   - Create training materials based on experiences from the 90 early-adopter branches
   - Identify "champion" branches that achieved above-average improvements to share best practices

3. **Monitor during rollout:**
   - Track service scores for 8–12 weeks after each branch switches
   - Expect similar ~2-point improvements; flag any branches that don't show gains for additional support

4. **Investigate variation:**
   - Some branches saw larger gains (up to +9 points) while others saw minimal change
   - Analyze what distinguished high-performing adopters to optimize the rollout

5. **Consider complementary improvements:**
   - Since staffing and visit levels didn't change, explore whether combining this procedure with other operational enhancements could yield additional gains

- **Data:** 2,550 weekly observations from 150 branches, weeks 6–22
- **Method:** Difference-in-differences regression with cluster-robust standard errors (clustered by branch)
- **Controls:** Staffing index and visits index included to account for other operational factors
