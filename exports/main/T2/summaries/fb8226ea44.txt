# **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

## **Bottom Line**

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure **improved service scores by approximately 2 points (4.3%)** compared to branches that kept the old procedure. This effect is statistically significant using standard methods, though the evidence is somewhat weaker when accounting for the fact that we have repeated observations from the same branches over time. The data supports proceeding with a broader rollout, but we recommend monitoring results closely.

---

## **Key Findings**

### Study Design
- **34 branches** participated in the study over 17 weeks (Weeks 6–22)
- **20 branches** adopted the new procedure; **14 branches** kept the old procedure
- The switch occurred in **Week 15**, giving us 9 weeks of "before" data and 8 weeks of "after" data
- This is a rigorous "difference-in-differences" design that compares changes in the treatment group to changes in the control group

### Results
| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| **Estimated effect** | +2.05 service score points |
| **Percentage improvement** | 4.3% above baseline |
| **Statistical significance** | p = 0.02 (standard); p = 0.11 (conservative) |
| **95% Confidence interval** | +0.3 to +3.8 points |

### What This Means
- Branches using the new procedure saw their service scores improve by about **2 points more** than branches using the old procedure
- The improvement is **modest but meaningful**—roughly equivalent to moving from the 50th to the 55th percentile of branch performance
- We verified that treatment and control branches had **similar trends before the switch**, which validates our comparison

### Important Caveats
1. **Statistical uncertainty**: When we use the most conservative statistical method (clustering by branch), the result is only marginally significant (p = 0.11). This means there's roughly a 1 in 10 chance the observed effect could be due to random variation.
2. **Short study period**: We only have 8 weeks of post-switch data. Longer-term effects (positive or negative) remain unknown.
3. **No major confounders**: The effect persists after controlling for staffing levels and visitor volume, suggesting the procedure change itself drove the improvement.

---

## **Next Steps**

### Recommended Actions

1. **Proceed with phased rollout** to remaining branches, but implement in waves to allow for ongoing evaluation

2. **Extend monitoring period** to at least 6 months post-rollout to confirm:
   - The effect persists over time
   - No unintended consequences emerge (e.g., staff burden, costs)

3. **Track implementation fidelity**—ensure branches are actually following the new procedure as designed

4. **Consider a cost-benefit analysis** comparing the 4.3% service score improvement against any additional labor or operational costs

5. **Set up ongoing dashboards** to monitor service scores across all branches and flag any that underperform after switching

---

## **Conclusion**

The evidence suggests the new procedure has a **positive but modest effect** on service quality. While not definitive, the results are encouraging enough to warrant a broader rollout with continued monitoring. The board should approve the expansion while requiring quarterly progress reports on service score trends.
