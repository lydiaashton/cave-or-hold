# **MEMORANDUM TO THE BOARD**
## **Evaluation of New Branch Rotation and Shelving Procedure**

### **BOTTOM LINE**

The data show that branches using the new procedure have higher service scores than those using the old procedure. However, **we cannot confidently attribute this improvement to the new procedure itself**. The branches selected to switch were already showing stronger score trends before the change. We recommend **continuing to monitor the situation for several more weeks** before making a systemwide rollout decision.

---

### **KEY FINDINGS**

**1. Observed Score Differences**
- Branches that switched to the new procedure averaged **52.98** after the switch, compared to **50.35** for branches that kept the old procedure—a gap of about 2.6 points.
- Using a standard before-and-after comparison, the new procedure appears associated with a **+1.9 point improvement** in service scores.

**2. Critical Limitation: Pre-Existing Trends**
- The branches chosen to switch were **already improving faster** than the control branches *before* the procedure change was implemented.
- In the weeks leading up to the switch (weeks 6–14), the treatment group gained approximately **0.37 points per week** relative to the control group (statistically significant, p < 0.001).
- When we account for this pre-existing trend, the estimated effect of the new procedure becomes **statistically indistinguishable from zero** (p = 0.55).

**3. Possible Delayed Effect**
- There is some suggestive evidence that the new procedure may have a **delayed positive effect**. In weeks 19–22 (5–8 weeks after the switch), the treatment branches showed a **2.2 point advantage** over what would be expected from pre-trends alone (p = 0.014).
- However, the first four weeks after the switch showed no detectable effect.

**4. Study Design**
- **425 branches** adopted the new procedure; **284 branches** continued with the old procedure.
- Data cover **17 weeks** (weeks 6–22), with the switch occurring at week 15.
- The analysis controls for staffing levels and visit volumes, which also correlate with service scores.

---

### **NEXT STEPS**

1. **Do not proceed with systemwide rollout at this time.** The evidence is not strong enough to justify the cost and disruption of changing procedures at all 709 branches.

2. **Continue data collection for 8–12 more weeks.** The delayed effect pattern (if real) needs more time to confirm. Additional weeks will also help determine whether the late-post advantage persists or fades.

3. **Consider a randomized rollout for future evaluation.** If the cooperative plans to test other procedural changes, randomly assigning branches to treatment and control groups would eliminate the pre-trend problem and produce more reliable estimates.

4. **Investigate why switching branches were outperforming.** Understanding what made these 425 branches different (management quality, community factors, etc.) could help target the new procedure to branches most likely to benefit.

5. **Revisit this decision in 2–3 months** with the additional data. If the late-post advantage holds or grows, a phased rollout may be warranted.

---

### **ANALYTICAL NOTE**

This analysis used a difference-in-differences approach, which requires that treatment and control groups would have followed parallel trends in the absence of the intervention. The pre-switch data show this assumption is violated, which biases the naive estimate upward. The adjusted analysis accounts for this but has less statistical power. More post-switch data will improve our ability to detect a true effect if one exists.
