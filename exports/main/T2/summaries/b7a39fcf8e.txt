# **Evaluation of New Branch Stock Rotation Procedure**

## **Bottom Line**

The new stock rotation and shelving procedure shows a **positive but statistically inconclusive effect** on service scores. Branches that switched saw service scores increase by approximately **2.1 points** (about 4% improvement) compared to branches that kept the old procedure. However, this result is not statistically significant at conventional levels, meaning we cannot confidently rule out that the observed improvement was due to random variation. **We recommend a cautious, phased rollout with continued monitoring rather than an immediate system-wide implementation.**

---

## **Key Findings**

### **Study Design**
- **34 branches** total: 20 adopted the new procedure (treatment group), 14 kept the old procedure (control group)
- **17 weeks of data** (weeks 6–22), with the switch implemented at week 15
- Analysis uses a difference-in-differences approach, comparing changes in treatment branches to changes in control branches

### **Results**
| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Estimated effect on service score | **+2.1 points** |
| Statistical significance | **Not significant** (p ≈ 0.10–0.12) |
| 95% Confidence interval | **-0.5 to +4.8 points** |
| Effect size (if real) | **~4% improvement** over baseline |

### **What the Data Shows**
1. **Treatment branches started with higher scores** (average 53.8 vs. 46.5 for control branches before the switch). This is accounted for in the analysis.

2. **After the switch**, treatment branches improved by 1.3 points while control branches declined by 0.8 points, yielding the 2.1-point difference.

3. **The confidence interval is wide**: The true effect could plausibly be zero, slightly negative, or as high as 5 points. With only 8 post-switch weeks and 34 branches, the study has limited statistical power.

4. **Pre-switch trends were parallel**: The gap between treatment and control branches was stable before week 15, supporting the validity of the comparison.

5. **Staffing and visit levels** are positively associated with service scores, but adjusting for these factors does not change the main conclusion.

---

## **Next Steps**

### **Recommended Actions**

1. **Continue the pilot for 8–12 more weeks** to gather additional post-switch data. This would improve statistical precision and help determine whether the 2-point improvement is real.

2. **Expand the pilot to 5–10 additional branches** if operationally feasible. More treatment branches would increase the power to detect meaningful effects.

3. **Monitor implementation fidelity**. Ensure branches that switched are actually following the new procedure as intended. Variation in implementation could dilute the measured effect.

4. **Track secondary outcomes**. Consider whether the new procedure affects other metrics (e.g., staff time, patron satisfaction, inventory accuracy) that might justify adoption even if service score effects are modest.

5. **Plan for phased rollout**. If results remain positive but inconclusive after extended monitoring, consider a staggered rollout that allows for ongoing evaluation and adjustment.

### **Decision Framework**

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|----------|----------------|
| Effect becomes statistically significant with more data | Proceed with system-wide rollout |
| Effect remains positive but inconclusive | Consider cost-benefit analysis; if implementation costs are low, phased rollout may be warranted |
| Effect diminishes or turns negative | Revisit procedure design before broader adoption |

---

## **Technical Notes**

- Analysis uses difference-in-differences regression with branch and week fixed effects
- Standard errors are clustered at the branch level to account for repeated observations
- Service scores average ~50 across all branches; higher scores indicate better service
- All 34 branches have complete data for all 17 weeks
