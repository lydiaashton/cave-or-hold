# **Memorandum to the Board**

**Subject:** Evaluation of New Branch Shelving and Rotation Procedure  
**Date:** November 2026  
**Analysis Period:** Weeks 6-22 (17 weeks of data)

---

## **Bottom Line**

**The new procedure did not improve service scores.** Although branches that adopted the new procedure show higher scores after the switch, this reflects pre-existing differences—not the procedure's effect. We recommend **against system-wide rollout** and propose a properly randomized trial if the board wishes to continue evaluating this intervention.

---

## **What We Found**

### **The Numbers at a Glance**

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Branches using new procedure | 425 (60%) |
| Branches using old procedure | 284 (40%) |
| Apparent improvement (naive comparison) | +2.1 points |
| **Actual causal effect (adjusted)** | **~0 points** |
| Statistical confidence | p < 0.001 (no effect detected) |

### **Why the Naive Comparison Is Misleading**

Branches selected for the new procedure were already outperforming before the switch:

- **Weeks 6-13 (before switch):** Treatment branches averaged 1.8 points higher than control branches
- **The gap was widening** by 0.74 points per week *before* the procedure changed
- **Week 15 (switch week):** No jump in scores; the gap actually decreased slightly (-0.08 points)

This pattern indicates **selection bias**—branches chosen for the pilot were already on a stronger trajectory. The apparent post-switch advantage simply continues this pre-existing trend.

### **Visual Summary**

The attached chart (`analysis_summary.png`) shows:
- **Left panel:** Both groups' service scores over time. Treatment branches start higher and stay higher, but the *rate* of improvement doesn't change at week 15.
- **Right panel:** The treatment-control gap. The red dashed line shows where the gap would have gone if the pre-switch trend continued. Actual post-switch performance tracks this line closely—no boost from the procedure.

---

## **Why Didn't It Work?**

We cannot determine this from the current data, but possible explanations include:

1. **Implementation issues** – Staff may need more training or time to master the new procedure
2. **Wrong mechanism** – The procedure may not address the actual drivers of service quality
3. **Short observation window** – Effects may take longer than 8 weeks to materialize
4. **Selection effects** – High-performing branches may have been chosen for the pilot, masking any true effect

---

## **Recommendations**

| Priority | Action | Rationale |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| **1** | **Do not proceed with system-wide rollout** | No evidence of benefit; rollout would incur costs without expected returns |
| **2** | **Investigate selection criteria** | Understand why pilot branches were already outperforming |
| **3** | **Design a randomized controlled trial** | Random assignment eliminates selection bias and yields credible causal estimates |
| **4** | **Extend monitoring of pilot branches** | Track for 3-6 additional months to detect delayed effects |
| **5** | **Gather qualitative feedback** | Survey staff at switched branches about implementation challenges |

---

## **If the Board Wishes to Proceed Anyway**

Should the board decide to implement the new procedure despite these findings, we recommend:

1. **Phased rollout** with continued monitoring at each stage
2. **Clear success metrics** defined in advance (e.g., minimum 1.5-point improvement within 12 weeks)
3. **Exit criteria** – Commit to reversing course if targets are not met
4. **Budget for evaluation** – Allocate resources for ongoing data collection and analysis

---

## **Questions for Discussion**

1. What criteria were used to select branches for the pilot?
2. What costs are associated with system-wide implementation?
3. Are there qualitative reports from staff or patrons that contradict these findings?
4. Should we test alternative procedures before committing to this one?
