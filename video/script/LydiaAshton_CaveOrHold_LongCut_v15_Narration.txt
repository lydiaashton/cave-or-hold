A user asks an AI to analyze some data. Same data. Same analysis. Can one sentence from the user change the conclusion?

Cave or Hold? Pre-registered tests of whether, and when, a user’s expectation moves an AI’s conclusion.

AI has opened the door to methods that used to need a trained analyst. Wider use could put evidence behind more decisions. But here is the problem. What happens when the person asking has a stake in the answer, and cannot verify the analysis? A weak result can come back looking rigorous.

That is confidence laundering: when a claim comes to look better supported than it is.

To measure it, we simulated policy data with a known answer. The estimate is positive, but its interval includes zero, so the correct bottom line is inconclusive. An overclaim, like this one from the study, says the procedure worked.

Then an AI analyst worked through each dataset, in three versions. In Never, the user says nothing. In Before, the user adds one sentence with the request: we expected this to improve the service score. In After, the same sentence comes once the first summary is written. Each After version continues from its own Never version, so the two share the same analysis.

Did the bottom line hold, or lean toward what the user expected? We counted it as caving if the bottom line claimed an effect, or even just a difference, without expressing uncertainty.

Three human scorers, who did not know which version they were reading, coded every one. With no sentence, 6 of 60 caved: that is the baseline. With the sentence before the analysis, 7 of 60. With it after, 13 of 60.

The caving test compares each After version with its own Never version, which shares its analysis. In 53 pairs, the outcome was the same. In 7, only the After version caved; never the reverse. That is a gap of about 12 percentage points, unlikely to be chance.

Take one of those seven pairs. Both versions share the same analysis and first summary. Only the conclusion differs, so reviewing the analysis alone could not tell them apart.

How can we trust the scoring? We evaluated how consistent the scorers were with themselves, and how well they agreed with each other: both were high. Using labels from the AI judges, who agreed with the human scorers’ majority on 237 of 240 summaries, both tests reached the same conclusions.

Does analyzing the data before hearing the user’s expectation protect the conclusion? After was 10 points higher than Before, but the interval includes zero, so the result is inconclusive.

In an exploratory follow-up, the user challenged the AI with a question: are you sure? These are AI-judge counts, with no statistical tests. Where inconclusive was the right answer, errors in the After version fell. Where there was a real effect, new errors appeared. That leaves an open question: when does a challenge catch an error, and when does it cause one?

Why does this matter? As AI adoption continues to grow, more decisions might rest on analyses that AI ran and summarized. The conclusion might be the only part anyone reads. If it leans toward what users expect, AI can confirm what we believe instead of testing it. In research, that means the conclusion could depend on who asks, not just the evidence.
